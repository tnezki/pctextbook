Chapter Introduction: Why This Chapter Matters

Trigonometry sits at the intersection of geometry, algebra, and calculus. In earlier courses, you learned about right-triangle trigonometry and basic angle measurement. Chapter 7 takes that foundation and extends it dramatically — from reading exact values off the unit circle to manipulating and proving identities, solving multi-step equations, and even laying the groundwork for integral calculus through area approximation.

The progression is intentional. Section 7.A builds the core language: exact values, reference angles, and the ASTC rule. Section 7.B introduces identities — algebraic laws of the trig world. Section 7.C expands your toolkit with sum, difference, and half-angle identities to reach angles the unit circle alone cannot provide. Section 7.D flips the script: now the angle is the unknown, and you must solve for it systematically. Finally, Section 7.E (Above & Beyond) introduces Riemann sums and the trapezoidal rule — the conceptual bridge to the definite integral.

Every section of this chapter is connected. Mastery of exact values (7.A) powers everything that follows. Identities (7.B, 7.C) give you the tools to simplify before solving. Equation-solving (7.D) is the culmination of trig skill in action. Area approximation (7.E) previews the single most important idea in all of calculus.

Key Vocabulary

Unit Circle A circle of radius 1 centered at the origin. Every point on it is $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$, directly defining sine and cosine for any angle $\theta$.
Reference Angle The acute angle between the terminal side of $\theta$ and the nearest x-axis. It gives the magnitude of any trig function; the quadrant determines the sign.
Coterminal Angles Angles that share the same terminal side on the unit circle. Found by adding or subtracting multiples of $2\pi$: $\theta \pm 2\pi k$.
ASTC Rule (All Students Take Calculus) A memory device for which trig functions are positive in each quadrant: All (QI), Sine (QII), Tangent (QIII), Cosine (QIV).
Trigonometric Identity An equation true for every value of the variable (where defined). Used as a substitution tool — not solved, but applied.
Pythagorean Identities Fundamental identities derived from $x^2 + y^2 = 1$: $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$, along with the tan/sec and cot/csc variations.
Double-Angle Identities Formulas expressing trig functions of $2\theta$ in terms of $\theta$: e.g., $\sin(2\theta) = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta$.
Half-Angle Identities Formulas expressing trig functions of $\frac{\theta}{2}$ using trig functions of $\theta$. Contain a $\pm$ sign determined by the quadrant of $\frac{\theta}{2}$, not $\theta$.
Sum Identity Formulas for $\sin(A+B)$ or $\cos(A+B)$ in terms of the sines and cosines of $A$ and $B$ separately.
Difference Identity Formulas for $\sin(A-B)$ or $\cos(A-B)$. Identical in structure to sum identities except one sign changes — and it changes differently for sine vs. cosine.
Principal Solutions Solutions to a trig equation that fall within one full period, typically $[0, 2\pi)$. The base from which all others are generated.
General Solution The complete infinite set of solutions expressed with $+\,2\pi n$ (sine/cosine) or $+\,\pi n$ (tangent), where $n$ is any integer.
Quadratic-Type Trig Equation A trig equation with a squared function, like $2\sin^2\theta - 1 = 0$. Solved by factoring or square roots, treating the trig function as the variable.
Riemann Sum An approximation of the area under a curve by summing the areas of rectangles whose heights are function values at left endpoints (left sum), right endpoints (right sum), or midpoints.
Left/Right Riemann Sum Left sum uses the function value at the left edge of each subinterval; right sum uses the right edge. For increasing functions, left underestimates and right overestimates.
Trapezoidal Rule An approximation method using trapezoids instead of rectangles, averaging the left and right function values: $T = \frac{\Delta x}{2}[f(x_0) + 2f(x_1) + \cdots + 2f(x_{n-1}) + f(x_n)]$.

Formula Reference Sheet

7.A — Unit Circle and Reciprocal Functions
$$\cos\theta = x \qquad \sin\theta = y \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}$$ $$\csc\theta = \frac{1}{\sin\theta} \qquad \sec\theta = \frac{1}{\cos\theta} \qquad \cot\theta = \frac{1}{\tan\theta} = \frac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}$$

Reference angles by quadrant:   QII: $\pi - \theta$  |  QIII: $\theta - \pi$  |  QIV: $2\pi - \theta$

7.B — Pythagorean and Double-Angle Identities
$$\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1 \qquad 1 + \tan^2\theta = \sec^2\theta \qquad 1 + \cot^2\theta = \csc^2\theta$$ $$\sin(2\theta) = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta$$ $$\cos(2\theta) = \cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta = 1 - 2\sin^2\theta = 2\cos^2\theta - 1$$ $$\tan(2\theta) = \frac{2\tan\theta}{1 - \tan^2\theta}$$
7.C — Sum, Difference, and Half-Angle Identities
$$\sin(A \pm B) = \sin A\cos B \pm \cos A\sin B$$ $$\cos(A + B) = \cos A\cos B - \sin A\sin B \qquad \cos(A - B) = \cos A\cos B + \sin A\sin B$$ $$\sin\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) = \pm\sqrt{\frac{1-\cos\theta}{2}} \qquad \cos\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) = \pm\sqrt{\frac{1+\cos\theta}{2}}$$ $$\tan\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) = \frac{\sin\theta}{1+\cos\theta} = \frac{1-\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}$$

The $\pm$ in half-angle formulas is determined by the quadrant of $\frac{\theta}{2}$, not $\theta$.

7.E — Riemann Sums and Trapezoidal Rule
$$L_n = \Delta x \left[f(x_0) + f(x_1) + \cdots + f(x_{n-1})\right] \quad \text{(Left Riemann Sum)}$$ $$R_n = \Delta x \left[f(x_1) + f(x_2) + \cdots + f(x_n)\right] \quad \text{(Right Riemann Sum)}$$ $$T_n = \frac{\Delta x}{2}\left[f(x_0) + 2f(x_1) + 2f(x_2) + \cdots + 2f(x_{n-1}) + f(x_n)\right] \quad \text{(Trapezoidal Rule)}$$

$\Delta x = \dfrac{b-a}{n}$ where $[a,b]$ is the interval and $n$ is the number of subintervals.

Chapter Summary

Section 7.A — Exact Trigonometric Values

  • The unit circle has radius 1 centered at the origin; every point is $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$. Cosine = x-coordinate; sine = y-coordinate — never reversed.
  • Memorize three Quadrant I pairs at $\frac{\pi}{6}, \frac{\pi}{4}, \frac{\pi}{3}$. Symmetry and the ASTC rule extend these to all four quadrants.
  • Reference angles give magnitude; the quadrant gives the sign. Subtract $2\pi$ to reduce large angles; add $2\pi$ to handle negative angles.
  • Reciprocal functions (csc, sec, cot) are computed by finding the primary function first, then taking its reciprocal.
  • $\tan\theta$ is undefined when $\cos\theta = 0$ (at $\frac{\pi}{2}$ and $\frac{3\pi}{2}$).

Section 7.B — Double-Angle and Pythagorean Identities

  • An identity is always true — use it as a substitution tool, not an equation to solve. The moment you see $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta$, replace it with 1.
  • The three Pythagorean identities all derive from $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ on the unit circle.
  • $\cos(2\theta)$ has three equivalent forms; choose the one that matches the given expression to avoid introducing new variables.
  • To simplify complex rational trig expressions, rewrite in terms of sine and cosine, then factor and cancel.

Section 7.C — Half-Angle, Sum, and Difference Identities

  • Sum/difference identities unlock exact values for non-standard angles (75°, 15°, 105°) by splitting them into two known special angles.
  • In sine sum/difference, the sign in the formula matches the $\pm$; in cosine, the sign flips.
  • Half-angle formulas contain a $\pm$ whose value is determined by the quadrant of $\frac{\theta}{2}$ — not $\theta$ itself. Always find $\frac{\theta}{2}$ first.
  • The half-angle tangent formulas ($\frac{\sin\theta}{1+\cos\theta}$ and $\frac{1-\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}$) require no $\pm$ sign — they are always exact.

Section 7.D — Solving Advanced Trigonometric Equations

  • The four-step strategy always applies: isolate the trig function → find the reference angle → identify quadrants using ASTC → list all solutions.
  • Trig equations typically have two solutions per period for sine and cosine, and one per period for tangent. The general solution captures all of them.
  • For quadratic-type equations, treat the trig function as the variable, factor or use the square root method, then solve each resulting equation.
  • For reciprocal function equations (sec, csc, cot), convert to sine or cosine first, then proceed with the standard strategy.
  • Always check solutions — squaring both sides or applying certain identities can introduce extraneous solutions.

Section 7.E — Riemann Sums and Trapezoidal Approximation

  • The area under a curve represents accumulated quantities: distance from velocity, total drug absorbed, total rainfall, etc.
  • Left Riemann sums underestimate when the function is increasing; right sums overestimate. These roles reverse for decreasing functions.
  • The trapezoidal rule averages the left and right sums and generally provides a better approximation than either alone.
  • More subintervals always improves accuracy. In the limit as $n \to \infty$, the Riemann sum becomes the definite integral $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$.
  • Always track units: if $f(x)$ is in mph and $x$ is in hours, the area has units of miles.

Chapter Conclusion: What You Now Know and Why It Matters

Chapter 7 represents one of the most mathematically dense and conceptually rich units in Precalculus. You began by grounding yourself in the unit circle — the single most important picture in all of trigonometry — and built upward from there. By the end, you are not just reading values from a memorized table; you are manipulating identities, solving equations with infinitely many solutions, and approximating quantities that can only be measured exactly in the limit.

Why does this matter? Every oscillating system in physics — a pendulum, a spring, a sound wave, an electrical circuit — is described by trigonometric functions. Every time an engineer designs a bridge, an architect accounts for vibration, or a musician understands harmonics, this mathematics is at work. The identities you practiced allow computers to simplify and evaluate trig expressions efficiently. The equation-solving skills you developed are exactly what calculus requires when optimizing functions or finding critical points.

Section 7.E pointed toward the future: the area under a curve, approximated first by rectangles, becomes the definite integral — the single most powerful tool in all of calculus. Every Riemann sum you computed in 7.E is a step toward that idea.

You have earned a complete and coherent understanding of trigonometry. These are not isolated facts — they are a connected system. Use them accordingly.

Section 7.A

1. $\sin\!\left(\dfrac{5\pi}{6}\right) = \dfrac{1}{2}$.   Reference angle $= \dfrac{\pi}{6}$; Quadrant II → sine is positive.
2. $\cos\!\left(\dfrac{7\pi}{4}\right) = \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$.   Reference angle $= \dfrac{\pi}{4}$; Quadrant IV → cosine is positive.
3. $\tan\!\left(\dfrac{4\pi}{3}\right) = \sqrt{3}$.   Reference angle $= \dfrac{\pi}{3}$; Quadrant III → tangent is positive.
4. $\csc\!\left(\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\right) = \dfrac{2}{\sqrt{3}} = \dfrac{2\sqrt{3}}{3}$.   $\sin\!\left(\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\right) = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$; csc is its reciprocal.
5. $\dfrac{17\pi}{6} - 2\pi = \dfrac{17\pi}{6} - \dfrac{12\pi}{6} = \dfrac{5\pi}{6}$.   $\cos\!\left(\dfrac{5\pi}{6}\right) = -\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ (Quadrant II; reference angle $\dfrac{\pi}{6}$; cosine is negative).
6. $\theta = \dfrac{5\pi}{4}$ is in Quadrant III; reference angle $= \dfrac{\pi}{4}$. Point: $\!\left(-\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2},\, -\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right)$.
$\sin = -\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$, $\cos = -\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$, $\tan = 1$, $\csc = -\sqrt{2}$, $\sec = -\sqrt{2}$, $\cot = 1$.

Section 7.B

7. $\cos\theta = \dfrac{4}{5}$ (QI, Pythagorean theorem on $3{-}4{-}5$ triangle). $\sin(2\theta) = 2\cdot\dfrac{3}{5}\cdot\dfrac{4}{5} = \dfrac{24}{25}$.
8. $\cos(2\theta) = 2\cos^2\theta - 1 = 2\!\left(\dfrac{1}{4}\right) - 1 = -\dfrac{1}{2}$.   (Or: $\cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta$ with $\sin\theta = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ gives the same result.)
9. $\dfrac{\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta}{\cos\theta} = \dfrac{1}{\cos\theta} = \sec\theta$.
10. $1 - \sin^2\theta = \cos^2\theta$   (from $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$).
11. $\cos\!\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2}\right) = 1 - 2\sin^2\!\left(\dfrac{\pi}{4}\right) = 1 - 2\!\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right)^{\!2} = 1 - 2\cdot\dfrac{1}{2} = 0$. ✓
12. $\dfrac{\tan\theta}{\sec\theta} = \dfrac{\sin\theta/\cos\theta}{1/\cos\theta} = \sin\theta$.
