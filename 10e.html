10.E Power Rule and Derivatives

Derivatives measure how a function changes. The power rule provides a fast and efficient way to differentiate polynomial and power functions.
By recognizing structure and simplifying expressions, derivatives can be computed quickly and applied to real-world problems involving rates of change.
“Derivatives reveal how change happens.”

Introduction

The derivative represents the rate at which a function changes. For many functions, especially polynomials, the power rule allows us to compute derivatives efficiently.

Instead of repeatedly using limits, we can apply patterns that simplify differentiation.

Key Idea: The power rule converts exponents into coefficients.

History

The concept of derivatives was developed by Newton and Leibniz in the 17th century as part of calculus. Their work allowed mathematicians to describe motion and change precisely.

The power rule emerged as a pattern from these discoveries and remains one of the most important tools in calculus.

Today, derivatives are used across science, engineering, and economics to analyze change.

Key Concepts

Power Rule

The power rule provides a direct way to differentiate power functions.

$$ \frac{d}{dx}(x^n)=nx^{n-1} $$

Example:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}(x^3)=3x^2 $$

Constant Multiple Rule

Constants remain as factors when differentiating.

$$ \frac{d}{dx}(c\,x^n)=c\cdot n x^{n-1} $$

Example:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}(4x^2)=8x $$

Derivative of a Constant

The derivative of a constant is zero because it does not change.

$$ \frac{d}{dx}(c)=0 $$

Example:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}(7)=0 $$

Polynomial Functions

Differentiate each term separately using the power rule.

$$ \frac{d}{dx}(ax^n + bx^m)=anx^{n-1}+bmx^{m-1} $$

Example:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}(3x^4 + 2x^2)=12x^3 + 4x $$

Negative Exponents

Negative exponents follow the same rule.

$$ \frac{d}{dx}(x^{-n})=-n x^{-n-1} $$

Example:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}(x^{-1})=-x^{-2} $$

Fractional Exponents

Fractional exponents represent roots and follow the same rule.

$$ \frac{d}{dx}(x^{1/2})=\frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2} $$

Example:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}(\sqrt{x})=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}} $$

Simplify Before Differentiating

Simplifying expressions first often makes differentiation easier.

$$ \frac{x^3}{x}=x^2 $$

Example:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{x^3}{x}\right)=\frac{d}{dx}(x^2)=2x $$

Interpreting Derivatives

The derivative represents the rate of change of a function.

$$ f'(x) = \text{rate of change} $$
Key Idea: Derivatives measure how fast a function changes.

Vocabulary

Derivative:

A measure of how a function changes with respect to its input.

Power Rule:

A rule used to differentiate functions of the form \(x^n\).

Polynomial Function:

A function made up of terms with nonnegative integer exponents.

Negative Exponent:

An exponent indicating reciprocal powers.

Fractional Exponent:

An exponent representing roots of a quantity.

Strategies

Using the Power Rule:

  1. Identify each term
  2. Apply the power rule to each term
  3. Simplify the result

Working with Exponents:

  1. Rewrite roots as fractional exponents
  2. Rewrite reciprocals as negative exponents
  3. Simplify before differentiating
Key Idea: Simplify first, then apply the power rule.

Common Mistakes

  • Forgetting the power rule: not multiplying by the exponent.
  • Not reducing the exponent: forgetting to subtract 1.
  • Dropping constants incorrectly: constants multiply through.
  • Not simplifying first: expressions like \( \frac{x^3}{x} \) should be simplified.
  • Errors with negative exponents: sign mistakes are common.
  • Errors with fractional exponents: they follow the same rule.
