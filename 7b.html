SECTION 7.B

Double-Angle and Pythagorean Identities

Trigonometric identities are the laws of the trig world — equations that are permanently, always, unconditionally true. Just as $a(b + c) = ab + ac$ works for every number you can imagine, an identity like $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$ holds for every angle in the universe. Learning to use these identities is less like memorizing facts and more like learning a new vocabulary — once you know the words, whole sentences of complicated math start to simplify themselves right before your eyes.
“ An identity is not a coincidence — it is a truth that holds for every single angle in existence. ”

Learning Objectives

  • I can apply double-angle identities for sine and cosine to evaluate and simplify expressions.
  • I can simplify trigonometric expressions using Pythagorean identities, including rational expressions.
  • I can recognize equivalent forms of identities, including negatives and multiples, and justify my reasoning.

Introduction: The Algebra of Trigonometry

In Section 7.A, we used the structure of the unit circle to read off exact values of sine, cosine, and tangent. That was like learning to read individual words. In this section, we learn to write sentences — we take trig expressions and rewrite them in different, simpler, or more useful forms using identities.

An identity is an equation that is true for every value of the variable (where the functions are defined). It is not an equation you solve — it is a tool you use to substitute. When you see $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta$ buried inside a complex expression, you do not solve for $\theta$. You simply replace the entire thing with $1$ and keep moving.

The Pythagorean identity $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$ is the most fundamental identity in all of trigonometry. It comes directly from the unit circle equation $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ — substituting $x = \cos\theta$ and $y = \sin\theta$ gives it to us immediately. Two more Pythagorean identities follow by dividing both sides by $\cos^2\theta$ or $\sin^2\theta$.

The double-angle identities answer a question that seems simple but is actually tricky: what is $\sin(2\theta)$? Your first instinct might be $2\sin\theta$ — but that is wrong. Doubling the angle is not the same as doubling the function value. The correct answer, $2\sin\theta\cos\theta$, is one of the most important formulas you will use in precalculus and beyond.

Perhaps the most interesting feature of this section is that $\cos(2\theta)$ can be written in three different but equally correct ways. This is not confusing — it is actually very convenient. Depending on what information you are given, one form will make your work much easier than the others.

📚 Historical Note: The Pythagorean identity $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$ takes its name from the Pythagorean theorem ($a^2 + b^2 = c^2$), proven by Greek mathematicians around 500 BC and attributed to the school of Pythagoras. When applied to a right triangle inscribed in the unit circle (hypotenuse $= 1$), the theorem gives the identity directly. The double-angle formulas appear in geometric form in Ptolemy's Almagest (c. 150 AD) and were written in modern algebraic notation by François Viète in the 1590s. Leonhard Euler (1707–1783) unified them into the notation we use today.
🔗 Connections to Other Subjects and Real Life:

Physics — Projectile Motion: The horizontal range formula for a projectile is $R = \frac{v^2 \sin(2\theta)}{g}$, where $\theta$ is the launch angle. The double-angle identity immediately reveals why the maximum range occurs at $\theta = 45°$: because $\sin(2 \cdot 45°) = \sin(90°) = 1$, its maximum possible value.

Electrical Engineering: In AC circuits, power calculations involve $\sin^2\theta$ and $\cos^2\theta$. The Pythagorean identity and power-reducing forms let engineers simplify these expressions without complicated integration.

Calculus: The identities $\sin^2\theta = \frac{1 - \cos(2\theta)}{2}$ and $\cos^2\theta = \frac{1 + \cos(2\theta)}{2}$ are essential for evaluating integrals. Without them, integrating $\sin^2\theta$ is impossible at the precalculus level — with them, it becomes straightforward.

Computer Graphics: Rotation matrices for spinning 3D objects contain $\sin\theta$ and $\cos\theta$. The double-angle formulas let graphics engines compute double-rotation transformations efficiently without calling the trig library twice.

Key Vocabulary and Concepts

Trigonometric Identity: An equation involving trig functions that is true for all values of the variable where the functions are defined. You do not solve an identity — you use it to substitute or rewrite part of a larger expression.
Pythagorean Identities: Three identities derived from the unit circle equation $x^2 + y^2 = 1$. The first is the foundation; the other two come from dividing the first by $\cos^2\theta$ or $\sin^2\theta$: $$\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$$ $$1 + \tan^2\theta = \sec^2\theta$$ $$1 + \cot^2\theta = \csc^2\theta$$
Double-Angle Identities: Formulas that express a trig function of $2\theta$ in terms of functions of $\theta$. There is one form for sine and three equivalent forms for cosine: $$\sin(2\theta) = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta$$ $$\cos(2\theta) = \cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta = 2\cos^2\theta - 1 = 1 - 2\sin^2\theta$$
Power-Reducing Identities: Rearrangements of the cosine double-angle formulas that express $\sin^2\theta$ and $\cos^2\theta$ in terms of $\cos(2\theta)$: $$\sin^2\theta = \frac{1 - \cos(2\theta)}{2} \qquad \cos^2\theta = \frac{1 + \cos(2\theta)}{2}$$ These are critical tools in calculus for integrating squared trig functions.

Where the Three Forms of $\cos(2\theta)$ Come From

Start with the first form: $\cos(2\theta) = \cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta$. Then substitute the Pythagorean identity in two different ways:

  • Replace $\sin^2\theta$ with $1 - \cos^2\theta$: $\cos^2\theta - (1 - \cos^2\theta) = 2\cos^2\theta - 1$   ← use when you know $\cos\theta$
  • Replace $\cos^2\theta$ with $1 - \sin^2\theta$: $(1 - \sin^2\theta) - \sin^2\theta = 1 - 2\sin^2\theta$   ← use when you know $\sin\theta$

Strategy for Simplifying Trig Expressions

✓ Four-Step Simplification Strategy:
  1. Look for a Pythagorean identity in the numerator or denominator. Replace it immediately.
  2. Expand double-angle expressions using the form that best matches what you have.
  3. Convert everything to sine and cosine if other approaches are not working.
  4. Factor and cancel only after fully substituting. Do not cancel individual terms — only cancel common factors.

⚠ Common Mistakes to Avoid

Mistake 1 — $\sin(2\theta) \neq 2\sin\theta$: This is the single most common error in this section. Doubling the angle is completely different from doubling the function. The correct formula requires both sine and cosine: $\sin(2\theta) = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta$. Check: $\sin(60°) = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$, but $2\sin(30°) = 2 \cdot \frac{1}{2} = 1$. They are not equal.


Mistake 2 — Misreading $\cos^2\theta$: The notation $\cos^2\theta$ means $(\cos\theta)^2$ — evaluate the function first, then square the result. It does NOT mean $\cos(\theta^2)$. These are completely different values.


Mistake 3 — Using the wrong form of $\cos(2\theta)$: All three forms are correct, but using the wrong one can make a problem much harder. If you know $\sin\theta$, use $1 - 2\sin^2\theta$. If you know $\cos\theta$, use $2\cos^2\theta - 1$. If you have both, use $\cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta$.


Mistake 4 — Treating identities like equations to solve: When you see $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta$, do not try to solve for $\theta$. Simply replace the expression with 1 and continue simplifying the larger problem.


Mistake 5 — Canceling terms instead of factors: In a fraction like $\frac{2\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta}{\cos^2\theta}$, you cannot cancel $\cos^2\theta$ from the numerator and denominator because $\cos^2\theta$ is a term in the numerator, not a factor. You can only cancel something that multiplies the entire numerator.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Given Value — Find $\sin(2\theta)$

Evaluate $\sin(2\theta)$ given that $\sin\theta = \dfrac{3}{5}$ and $\theta$ is in Quadrant I.

Step 1: Identify what you need.
The formula $\sin(2\theta) = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta$ requires both $\sin\theta$ and $\cos\theta$. We have $\sin\theta$, so we need to find $\cos\theta$.

Step 2: Use the Pythagorean identity to find $\cos\theta$.

$$\cos^2\theta = 1 - \sin^2\theta = 1 - \left(\frac{3}{5}\right)^2 = 1 - \frac{9}{25} = \frac{16}{25}$$ $$\cos\theta = \frac{4}{5} \quad \text{(positive in Quadrant I)}$$

Step 3: Apply the double-angle identity.

$$\sin(2\theta) = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta = 2 \cdot \frac{3}{5} \cdot \frac{4}{5} = \frac{24}{25}$$

Final Answer: $\sin(2\theta) = \dfrac{24}{25}$

Example 2: Choosing the Right Form — Rewrite $\cos(2\theta)$ in Terms of Sine Only

Rewrite $\cos(2\theta)$ in terms of sine only, then evaluate if $\sin\theta = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$.

Step 1: Select the sine-only form.

$$\cos(2\theta) = 1 - 2\sin^2\theta$$

Step 2: Substitute $\sin\theta = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$.

$$\cos(2\theta) = 1 - 2\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)^2 = 1 - 2 \cdot \frac{3}{4} = 1 - \frac{3}{2} = -\frac{1}{2}$$

Final Answer: $\cos(2\theta) = 1 - 2\sin^2\theta$; evaluated: $-\dfrac{1}{2}$

Note: We can verify — if $\sin\theta = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$, then $\theta = \frac{\pi}{3}$, so $2\theta = \frac{2\pi}{3}$, and $\cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{3}\right) = -\frac{1}{2}$. ✓

Example 3: Power-Reducing — Simplify $\dfrac{1 - \cos(2\theta)}{2}$

Simplify $\dfrac{1 - \cos(2\theta)}{2}$ completely.

Step 1: Recognize the power-reducing identity.
The expression $\dfrac{1 - \cos(2\theta)}{2}$ is the power-reducing identity for sine. We can verify this by working backwards from $\cos(2\theta) = 1 - 2\sin^2\theta$:

$$1 - \cos(2\theta) = 1 - (1 - 2\sin^2\theta) = 2\sin^2\theta$$

Step 2: Divide both sides by 2.

$$\frac{1 - \cos(2\theta)}{2} = \frac{2\sin^2\theta}{2} = \sin^2\theta$$

Final Answer: $\sin^2\theta$

This identity is used in calculus to integrate $\sin^2\theta$ — it converts a squared function into a form with no squares.

Example 4: Rational Expression — Simplify $\dfrac{\sin^2\theta}{1 - \cos^2\theta}$

Simplify $\dfrac{\sin^2\theta}{1 - \cos^2\theta}$ completely.

Step 1: Identify the Pythagorean identity in the denominator.
From $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$, rearrange: $1 - \cos^2\theta = \sin^2\theta$

Step 2: Substitute into the denominator.

$$\frac{\sin^2\theta}{1 - \cos^2\theta} = \frac{\sin^2\theta}{\sin^2\theta}$$

Step 3: Cancel.

$$= 1 \quad (\text{for all } \theta \text{ where } \sin\theta \neq 0)$$

Final Answer: 1

Example 5 (Extended): Multi-Step — Simplify $\dfrac{\sin(2\theta)}{\cos\theta}$

Simplify $\dfrac{\sin(2\theta)}{\cos\theta}$ completely.

Step 1: Expand the numerator using the double-angle identity.

$$\sin(2\theta) = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta$$

Step 2: Substitute.

$$\frac{\sin(2\theta)}{\cos\theta} = \frac{2\sin\theta\cos\theta}{\cos\theta}$$

Step 3: Cancel the common factor of $\cos\theta$.
(Valid when $\cos\theta \neq 0$, i.e., when $\theta \neq \frac{\pi}{2} + \pi k$)

$$= 2\sin\theta$$

Final Answer: $2\sin\theta$

Key insight: Always expand double-angle expressions in the numerator first — this reveals factors that can cancel with the denominator.

Example 6 (Extended): Harder — Prove $\dfrac{1 - \tan^2\theta}{1 + \tan^2\theta} = \cos(2\theta)$

Verify this identity by simplifying the left side until it matches the right side.

Step 1: Replace $\tan\theta$ with $\dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}$ throughout.

$$\frac{1 - \dfrac{\sin^2\theta}{\cos^2\theta}}{1 + \dfrac{\sin^2\theta}{\cos^2\theta}}$$

Step 2: Multiply numerator and denominator by $\cos^2\theta$ to clear fractions.

$$= \frac{\cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta}{\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta}$$

Step 3: Apply identities.
Numerator: $\cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta = \cos(2\theta)$
Denominator: $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$

$$= \frac{\cos(2\theta)}{1} = \cos(2\theta) \quad \checkmark$$

Identity verified.

Strategy: When you see $1 + \tan^2\theta$ in a denominator, recognize it as $\sec^2\theta$, or convert to sines and cosines and multiply through. Both paths work.

Summary of Key Concepts

  • A trigonometric identity is always true. Use it to substitute, not to solve.
  • The fundamental Pythagorean identity is $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$, derived directly from the unit circle.
  • Two more Pythagorean identities follow by dividing by $\cos^2\theta$ ($\Rightarrow 1 + \tan^2\theta = \sec^2\theta$) or $\sin^2\theta$ ($\Rightarrow 1 + \cot^2\theta = \csc^2\theta$).
  • $\sin(2\theta) = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta$ — never $2\sin\theta$. You need both functions.
  • $\cos(2\theta)$ has three equivalent forms. Choose the one that matches your given information to save work.
  • The power-reducing identities — $\sin^2\theta = \frac{1-\cos(2\theta)}{2}$ and $\cos^2\theta = \frac{1+\cos(2\theta)}{2}$ — are rearrangements of the double-angle cosine formulas.
  • When given $\sin\theta$ or $\cos\theta$ in a specific quadrant, find the missing function with the Pythagorean identity before applying double-angle formulas.
  • When simplifying rational expressions, look for Pythagorean identities first, then expand, then factor and cancel.
  • To verify an identity: simplify one side until it matches the other. Do not move terms across the equals sign.

Answer Key

1. $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$. This is the fundamental Pythagorean identity. It equals 1 for every value of $\theta$.
2. $\cos(2\theta) = 2\cos^2\theta - 1$. Derived by substituting $\sin^2\theta = 1 - \cos^2\theta$ into $\cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta$: $\cos^2\theta - (1 - \cos^2\theta) = 2\cos^2\theta - 1$.
3. $\cos(2\theta) = 1 - 2\sin^2\theta$. Derived by substituting $\cos^2\theta = 1 - \sin^2\theta$ into $\cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta$: $(1 - \sin^2\theta) - \sin^2\theta = 1 - 2\sin^2\theta$.
4. $\dfrac{1 + \cos(2\theta)}{2} = \cos^2\theta$. This is the power-reducing identity, rearranged from $\cos(2\theta) = 2\cos^2\theta - 1 \Rightarrow \cos^2\theta = \dfrac{1 + \cos(2\theta)}{2}$.

5. Find $\sin\theta$ first: $\sin^2\theta = 1 - \left(\frac{4}{5}\right)^2 = 1 - \frac{16}{25} = \frac{9}{25}$, so $\sin\theta = \frac{3}{5}$ (positive, QI).

$\sin(2\theta) = 2 \cdot \frac{3}{5} \cdot \frac{4}{5} = \dfrac{24}{25}$

6. $\dfrac{\sin^2\theta}{1 - \cos^2\theta}$: The denominator $1 - \cos^2\theta = \sin^2\theta$ by the Pythagorean identity. So $\dfrac{\sin^2\theta}{\sin^2\theta} = 1$.
7. $\dfrac{1 + \tan^2\theta}{\sec^2\theta}$: The numerator $1 + \tan^2\theta = \sec^2\theta$ by the second Pythagorean identity. So $\dfrac{\sec^2\theta}{\sec^2\theta} = 1$.

8. Use the sine-only form: $\cos(2\theta) = 1 - 2\sin^2\theta$.

$\cos(2\theta) = 1 - 2\left(-\frac{3}{5}\right)^2 = 1 - 2 \cdot \frac{9}{25} = 1 - \frac{18}{25} = \dfrac{7}{25}$

Note: The quadrant does not affect this calculation because $\sin^2\theta$ is always positive.

9. $\dfrac{\sin(2\theta)}{\cos\theta}$: Expand the numerator: $\sin(2\theta) = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta$.

$\dfrac{2\sin\theta\cos\theta}{\cos\theta} = 2\sin\theta$   (valid when $\cos\theta \neq 0$)

10. $\dfrac{\sin^2\theta - \cos^2\theta}{\cos(2\theta)}$: The numerator $\sin^2\theta - \cos^2\theta = -(\cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta) = -\cos(2\theta)$.

$\dfrac{-\cos(2\theta)}{\cos(2\theta)} = -1$

11. Factor as difference of squares: $\cos^4\theta - \sin^4\theta = (\cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta)(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta)$

$= (\cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta)(1) = \cos(2\theta)$

12. $\dfrac{2\sin\theta\cos\theta}{1 - 2\sin^2\theta}$: Numerator $= \sin(2\theta)$; Denominator $= \cos(2\theta)$.

$\dfrac{\sin(2\theta)}{\cos(2\theta)} = \tan(2\theta)$

13. $\cos\theta = -\frac{8}{17}$, Quadrant II.

Find $\sin\theta$: $\sin^2\theta = 1 - \frac{64}{289} = \frac{225}{289}$, so $\sin\theta = \frac{15}{17}$ (positive in QII).

$\sin(2\theta) = 2 \cdot \frac{15}{17} \cdot \left(-\frac{8}{17}\right) = -\dfrac{240}{289}$

The answer is negative because $2\theta$ lands in Quadrant III or IV when $\theta$ is in QII, and we can verify the sign matches.

14. Start from $\cos(2\theta) = \cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta$. Divide numerator and denominator by $\cos^2\theta$:

$$\cos(2\theta) = \frac{\cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta}{\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta} \cdot \frac{1/\cos^2\theta}{1/\cos^2\theta} = \frac{1 - \tan^2\theta}{\sec^2\theta} = \frac{1 - \tan^2\theta}{1 + \tan^2\theta}$$

15. All three forms start from $\cos(2\theta) = \cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta$. To get the cosine-only form, substitute $\sin^2\theta = 1 - \cos^2\theta$: $\cos^2\theta - (1 - \cos^2\theta) = 2\cos^2\theta - 1$. To get the sine-only form, substitute $\cos^2\theta = 1 - \sin^2\theta$: $(1 - \sin^2\theta) - \sin^2\theta = 1 - 2\sin^2\theta$. Use the mixed form when you have both functions. Use the cosine-only form when only $\cos\theta$ is given. Use the sine-only form when only $\sin\theta$ is given — it avoids the extra step of finding $\cos\theta$.

16. The student's error is writing $\sin(2 \cdot 30°) = 2\sin(30°)$. This incorrectly assumes $\sin(2\theta) = 2\sin\theta$, which is false. The correct formula is $\sin(2\theta) = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta$. Applied to $\theta = 30°$: $\sin(60°) = 2\sin(30°)\cos(30°) = 2 \cdot \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$. The student's answer of 1 is incorrect; the true value $\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \approx 0.866$. The general rule $\sin(2\theta) = 2\sin\theta$ fails because trig functions do not distribute over angle multiplication — you must use the identity.
