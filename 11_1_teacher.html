Warm-Up

Use the questions below to bring forward ideas from algebra, trigonometry, and the meaning of derivative before beginning the section.

1. Rewrite each expression using exponent notation.

  • $\sqrt{x}$
  • $\sqrt[3]{x^2}$
  • $\dfrac{1}{x^4}$

Answer: $\sqrt{x}=x^{1/2}$, $\sqrt[3]{x^2}=x^{2/3}$, and $\dfrac{1}{x^4}=x^{-4}$.

2. Evaluate each trigonometric expression.

  • $\sin 0$
  • $\cos 0$
  • $\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2}\right)$
  • $\cos(\pi)$

Answer: $\sin 0=0$, $\cos 0=1$, $\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2}\right)=1$, and $\cos(\pi)=-1$.

3. In words, what does it mean to say that a derivative gives the instantaneous rate of change at a point?

Sample Answer: It describes how fast a function is changing at one exact input value. On a graph, it is the slope of the tangent line at that point.

4. Sketch a curve and draw a tangent line at one point. What does the slope of that tangent line represent?

Sample Answer: The slope of the tangent line represents the derivative at that point, or the instantaneous rate of change there.

Intro

Directions

Read the introduction aloud with a partner or in a small group. As you read, annotate the text by marking important ideas, circling key vocabulary, and writing short notes about connections, questions, or predictions in the margins.

This section begins with an idea that has already mattered in earlier work: a derivative tells how a function is changing at a single instant and connects to the slope of a tangent line. That meaning stays central here, but the focus now begins to shift toward efficient computation. The power rule offers a first major shortcut for differentiating expressions built from powers of $x$, while the derivatives of sine and cosine extend that work into familiar trigonometric territory. Together, these ideas start to turn the derivative from a concept students can describe into a tool they can actually use.

The reasoning in this section connects naturally to work beyond precalculus. In physics, rates of change describe motion, velocity, and the behavior of changing systems. In computer science and data analysis, recognizing structure and using efficient rules often matters more than repeating a long process from scratch each time. This section highlights that same habit of mind: looking closely at structure, deciding what kind of object is in front of you, and choosing a strategy that fits.

These functions also appear in models of real phenomena. Powers of $x$ show up in relationships involving growth, area, volume, and motion, while sine and cosine appear in circular motion, waves, sound, seasonal patterns, and other repeating behavior. When those functions are differentiated, they help describe how the modeled quantity is changing at a given instant. In that way, the derivative becomes more than a classroom procedure; it becomes a way of describing change in systems that students can actually imagine or observe.

Historically, the ideas in this section come from different mathematical traditions that later met through calculus. Powers and exponent rules grew out of algebraic work, while sine and cosine were developed through geometry, astronomy, and trigonometry. Calculus brought those worlds together by asking a new kind of question: not only what a function is, but how it changes. The power rule became one of the earliest and most useful shortcuts in differential calculus, and the derivatives of sine and cosine helped show that trigonometric functions could also be studied through the language of change.

Notation Reminder

Common Derivative Notation

  • $f'(x)$
  • $y'$
  • $\dfrac{d}{dx}$

These symbols all represent derivative notation. In this section, you will see and use all three forms.

The Power Rule

Power Rule

\[ \frac{d}{dx}(x^n)=nx^{n-1} \]

To differentiate a power of $x$, multiply by the exponent and then subtract $1$ from the exponent.

This is the first major shortcut in the section and gives an efficient way to differentiate polynomial terms written as powers of $x$.

Stop and Discuss

Why is the power rule helpful?

Answer: The power rule gives a fast way to differentiate powers of $x$.

Example 1

Find the derivative of $f(x)=x^6$.

Answer: By the power rule, $f'(x)=6x^5$.

You Try It 1

Find the derivative of $g(x)=x^8$.

Answer: $g'(x)=8x^7$.

Rewrite Before You Differentiate

Some expressions are easier to differentiate after rewriting them in exponent form.

Useful Rewrites

\[ \sqrt{x}=x^{1/2} \] \[ \sqrt[3]{x^2}=x^{2/3} \] \[ \frac{1}{x^4}=x^{-4} \]

Stop and Discuss

Why do we often rewrite expressions in exponent form before differentiating?

Answer: Rewriting expressions in exponent form often makes the derivative easier to find.

Example 2

Find the derivative of $h(x)=2\sqrt{x}-\dfrac{3}{x^2}$.

Answer: Rewrite first: $h(x)=2x^{1/2}-3x^{-2}$.

\[ h'(x)=2\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)x^{-1/2}-3(-2)x^{-3}=x^{-1/2}+6x^{-3} \]

You Try It 2

Find the derivative of $m(x)=3\sqrt{x}+\dfrac{4}{x^3}$.

Answer: Rewrite first: $m(x)=3x^{1/2}+4x^{-3}$.

\[ m'(x)=3\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)x^{-1/2}+4(-3)x^{-4}=\frac{3}{2}x^{-1/2}-12x^{-4} \]

The Derivatives of Sine and Cosine

Sine and cosine do not use the power rule. They have their own basic derivative facts.

Basic Trigonometric Derivatives

\[ \frac{d}{dx}(\sin x)=\cos x \] \[ \frac{d}{dx}(\cos x)=-\sin x \]

Stop and Discuss

What two trigonometric derivative facts do we need to know in this section?

Answer: The basic trigonometric derivatives are $\dfrac{d}{dx}(\sin x)=\cos x$ and $\dfrac{d}{dx}(\cos x)=-\sin x$.

Example 3

Find the derivative of $p(x)=3\sin x-4\cos x$.

Answer:

\[ p'(x)=3\cos x+4\sin x \]

You Try It 3

Find the derivative of $q(x)=5\sin x+2\cos x$.

Answer:

\[ q'(x)=5\cos x-2\sin x \]

Combining Power Terms with Sine and Cosine

When an expression is built from terms that are added or subtracted, each term can be differentiated separately. That lets the power rule and the trig derivative facts work together in one problem.

Strategy

  1. Rewrite any radicals or quotients if needed.
  2. Identify which terms are powers of $x$.
  3. Identify which terms are sine or cosine terms.
  4. Differentiate term by term.
  5. Simplify the result.

Stop and Discuss

How are mixed expressions with power terms and trig terms differentiated in this section?

Answer: Mixed expressions can be differentiated term by term when the terms are added or subtracted.

Example 4

Find the derivative of $r(x)=5x^3-2\sin x+7\cos x$.

Answer:

\[ r'(x)=15x^2-2\cos x-7\sin x \]

You Try It 4

Find the derivative of $s(x)=6x^2+4\sin x-3\cos x$.

Answer:

\[ s'(x)=12x+4\cos x+3\sin x \]

Summary Concepts

  • The derivative still represents instantaneous rate of change at a point.
  • The power rule gives a fast way to differentiate powers of $x$.
  • Rewriting expressions in exponent form often makes the derivative easier to find.
  • The basic trigonometric derivatives are $\dfrac{d}{dx}(\sin x)=\cos x$ and $\dfrac{d}{dx}(\cos x)=-\sin x$.
  • Mixed expressions can be differentiated term by term when the terms are added or subtracted.

Common Mistakes

  • Forgetting that $\dfrac{d}{dx}(\cos x)=-\sin x$.
  • Forgetting to rewrite radicals or reciprocal powers before differentiating.
  • Not treating added and subtracted terms separately.

Detailed Example Solutions

Example 1 Solution

Find the derivative of $f(x)=x^6$.

Apply the power rule.

\[ f'(x)=6x^5 \]

Example 2 Solution

Find the derivative of $h(x)=2\sqrt{x}-\dfrac{3}{x^2}$.

Rewrite the function first.

\[ h(x)=2x^{1/2}-3x^{-2} \]

Differentiate each term.

\[ h'(x)=2\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)x^{-1/2}-3(-2)x^{-3} \] \[ h'(x)=x^{-1/2}+6x^{-3} \]

Example 3 Solution

Find the derivative of $p(x)=3\sin x-4\cos x$.

Use the trig derivative facts term by term.

\[ p'(x)=3\cos x+4\sin x \]

Example 4 Solution

Find the derivative of $r(x)=5x^3-2\sin x+7\cos x$.

Differentiate each term separately and simplify.

\[ r'(x)=15x^2-2\cos x-7\sin x \]
