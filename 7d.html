SECTION 7.D

Solving Advanced Trigonometric Equations

Solving a trigonometric equation combines everything you have learned about the unit circle, reference angles, quadrant signs, and algebraic manipulation — all in one problem. Unlike a simple algebraic equation where $x = 3$ might be the only answer, a trig equation can have infinitely many solutions because trig functions repeat. Learning to find all of them, precisely and systematically, is the goal of this section.
“ Every equation is a question. The unit circle already knows the answer — your job is to listen carefully. ”

Learning Objectives

  • I can solve basic trigonometric equations for all six trig functions in $[0, 2\pi)$.
  • I can solve linear trigonometric equations in radians involving secant, cosecant, and cotangent.
  • I can solve advanced trigonometric equations in radians, including quadratic and double-angle types, and verify solutions.
  • I can write general solutions using correct period notation.

Introduction: When Angles Are Unknowns

In Sections 7.A through 7.C, the angle was always given — your job was to find the trig value. Now we flip that around. The trig value is given, and your job is to find the angle. This is the essence of solving a trigonometric equation.

Consider the equation $\sin\theta = \frac{1}{2}$. From the unit circle, you know that $\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{6}\right) = \frac{1}{2}$. But sine is also $\frac{1}{2}$ at $\theta = \frac{5\pi}{6}$, because both angles are in quadrants where sine is positive and share the same reference angle. That gives us two solutions in $[0, 2\pi)$. And since sine repeats every $2\pi$, there are actually infinitely many solutions — all described by the general solution.

The four-step strategy for solving any trig equation is always the same: isolate the trig function, find the reference angle, identify all quadrants where the function has the correct sign, and then list every solution in the interval (or write the general form). The algebra at the beginning may look different depending on the equation type, but the core process never changes.

This section also introduces equations involving the reciprocal functions — secant, cosecant, and cotangent. The key insight for all three is to convert to sine or cosine first, then solve as usual. These equations are not harder — they just have one extra translation step at the beginning.

🌎 Real-World Application — Ferris Wheel Height:

Suppose the height (in feet) of a rider on a Ferris wheel is modeled by: $$h(t) = 20 + 18\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{6}t\right)$$ where $t$ is time in minutes. To find when the rider is exactly 29 feet above the ground, we solve: $$20 + 18\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{6}t\right) = 29$$ $$\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{6}t\right) = \frac{1}{2}$$ This is a trig equation. Solving it tells us the exact times the rider reaches 29 feet — not just once, but every time around the wheel. This section gives you the tools to solve exactly this kind of problem.
📚 Historical Note: Solving trigonometric equations became important in the 17th century alongside the study of oscillatory motion — pendulums, waves, and planetary orbits. Galileo Galilei (1564–1642) noticed that pendulums swing in regular time intervals, and Isaac Newton (1643–1727) used trigonometric equations to model gravitational and wave phenomena. The concept of a general solution — using integer $n$ to represent infinite periodic repetitions — was formalized as algebra and trigonometry merged into modern mathematical notation during the 18th century.
🔗 Connections to Other Subjects and Real Life:

Physics — Simple Harmonic Motion: Any oscillating system (spring, pendulum, sound wave) is modeled by $x(t) = A\sin(\omega t + \phi)$. Solving for specific positions or times requires solving trig equations like those in this section.

Electrical Engineering: AC voltage alternates as $V(t) = V_0\sin(2\pi f t)$. Engineers solve trig equations to find the exact times when voltage crosses a threshold — for example, when safety cutoffs should activate.

Astronomy: The position of a planet in its orbit as a function of time involves solving trig equations. Astronomers use these to predict eclipses, conjunctions, and the timing of seasonal events.

Calculus: Finding where a trig function equals zero or reaches a maximum/minimum — critical for optimization problems — requires solving trig equations. This section builds the exact skill needed for that.

Key Vocabulary and Concepts

Principal Solutions: The solutions to a trig equation that fall within one complete cycle, typically $0 \leq \theta < 2\pi$. These are the "base" solutions from which all others are generated.
General Solution: The complete set of all solutions to a trig equation, expressed using $+ n$ notation where $n$ is any integer. The period of the function determines the spacing: sine and cosine use $+\, 2\pi n$, while tangent uses $+\, \pi n$.
Reference Angle: The acute angle between the terminal side of $\theta$ and the x-axis. In a trig equation, once you know the reference angle from the given value, you find all quadrant solutions by applying ASTC.
Quadratic-Type Trig Equation: An equation where a trig function is squared, such as $2\sin^2\theta - 1 = 0$. These are solved like quadratic equations — by factoring or taking square roots — except the variable is a trig function rather than $x$.

The Four-Step Solving Strategy

✓ Strategy for Solving Any Trig Equation:
  1. Isolate the trig function. Use algebra to get the trig function alone on one side. If the equation involves sec, csc, or cot, rewrite in terms of cos, sin, or tan first.
  2. Find the reference angle. Use the unit circle to identify the acute angle whose trig value matches the absolute value of the right side.
  3. Identify all quadrants. Use the sign of the right side and the ASTC rule to determine which quadrants contain solutions.
  4. List all solutions in the interval. Write the angle in each correct quadrant. If a general solution is required, append $+\, 2\pi n$ (or $+\, \pi n$ for tangent/cotangent).

⚠ Common Mistakes to Avoid

Mistake 1 — Finding only one solution: Most trig equations have two solutions in $[0, 2\pi)$. After finding the reference angle, always check both quadrants where the sign is correct. For example, $\sin\theta = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ has solutions in both QI and QII: $\frac{\pi}{4}$ and $\frac{3\pi}{4}$.


Mistake 2 — Using the wrong period for general solutions: Sine and cosine repeat every $2\pi$, so general solutions use $+ 2\pi n$. Tangent (and cotangent) repeat every $\pi$, so their general solutions use $+ \pi n$. Using $2\pi n$ for tangent will miss half of the solutions.


Mistake 3 — Leaving answers in degrees when radians are required: This course works in radians. Always express final answers as fractions of $\pi$, not as decimal degrees. $\frac{\pi}{3}$ is correct; $60°$ is not the expected form.


Mistake 4 — Dividing by a trig function instead of factoring: If you have $\cos\theta(2\sin\theta - 1) = 0$, do not divide both sides by $\cos\theta$. You would lose the solutions where $\cos\theta = 0$. Instead, set each factor equal to zero separately.


Mistake 5 — Double-angle equations: forgetting to divide by 2 at the end: When solving $\cos(2\theta) = 0$, substitute $u = 2\theta$ and solve for $u$ first (getting values in $[0, 4\pi)$ since $u = 2\theta$ can range up to $4\pi$). Then divide each $u$ value by 2 to get $\theta$. Students often forget the last step.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Basic Sine Equation — Solve $\sin\theta = \dfrac{1}{2}$ for $0 \leq \theta < 2\pi$

Step 1: The trig function is already isolated.

Step 2: Find the reference angle.
$\sin^{-1}\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = \frac{\pi}{6}$   (the reference angle is $\frac{\pi}{6}$)

Step 3: Determine quadrants.
The right side is positive ($+\frac{1}{2}$), so we need quadrants where sine is positive: QI and QII.

Step 4: Write solutions.

  • QI: $\theta = \frac{\pi}{6}$
  • QII: $\theta = \pi - \frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{5\pi}{6}$

Final Answer: $\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{6},\; \dfrac{5\pi}{6}$

General solution: $\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{6} + 2\pi n$ and $\theta = \dfrac{5\pi}{6} + 2\pi n$, where $n$ is any integer.

Example 2: Linear Equation — Solve $2\cos\theta - 1 = 0$ for $0 \leq \theta < 2\pi$

Step 1: Isolate the trig function.

$$2\cos\theta = 1 \implies \cos\theta = \frac{1}{2}$$

Step 2: Find the reference angle.
$\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{3}\right) = \frac{1}{2}$, so the reference angle is $\frac{\pi}{3}$.

Step 3: Cosine is positive in QI and QIV.

Step 4: Write solutions.

  • QI: $\theta = \frac{\pi}{3}$
  • QIV: $\theta = 2\pi - \frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{5\pi}{3}$

Final Answer: $\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{3},\; \dfrac{5\pi}{3}$

Example 3: Reciprocal Function — Solve $\sec\theta = 2$ for $0 \leq \theta < 2\pi$

Step 1: Rewrite in terms of cosine.
$\sec\theta = 2 \implies \cos\theta = \dfrac{1}{2}$

Step 2: Reference angle.
$\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{3}\right) = \frac{1}{2}$, so reference angle $= \frac{\pi}{3}$.

Step 3: Cosine is positive in QI and QIV.

Step 4: Write solutions.

  • QI: $\theta = \frac{\pi}{3}$
  • QIV: $\theta = 2\pi - \frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{5\pi}{3}$

Final Answer: $\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{3},\; \dfrac{5\pi}{3}$

Example 4: Quadratic-Type — Solve $2\sin^2\theta - 1 = 0$ for $0 \leq \theta < 2\pi$

Step 1: Isolate $\sin^2\theta$.

$$2\sin^2\theta = 1 \implies \sin^2\theta = \frac{1}{2}$$

Step 2: Take the square root (include $\pm$).

$$\sin\theta = \pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = \pm\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$$

Step 3: Solve each case.

Case 1: $\sin\theta = +\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ → Reference angle $= \frac{\pi}{4}$ → QI and QII:
$\theta = \frac{\pi}{4}$ and $\theta = \frac{3\pi}{4}$

Case 2: $\sin\theta = -\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ → Reference angle $= \frac{\pi}{4}$ → QIII and QIV:
$\theta = \pi + \frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{5\pi}{4}$ and $\theta = 2\pi - \frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{7\pi}{4}$

Final Answer: $\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{4},\; \dfrac{3\pi}{4},\; \dfrac{5\pi}{4},\; \dfrac{7\pi}{4}$

Note: Four solutions because the $\pm$ creates two separate equations, each with two quadrant solutions.

Example 5 (Extended): Double-Angle — Solve $\cos(2\theta) = 0$ for $0 \leq \theta < 2\pi$

Step 1: Substitute $u = 2\theta$ to simplify.
$\cos(u) = 0$

Step 2: Determine the range of $u$.
Since $0 \leq \theta < 2\pi$, multiplying by 2 gives $0 \leq u < 4\pi$. We need all solutions in $[0, 4\pi)$.

Step 3: Solve $\cos(u) = 0$.
Cosine equals zero at $\frac{\pi}{2}$ and $\frac{3\pi}{2}$ in $[0, 2\pi)$. Extending to $[0, 4\pi)$:

$$u = \frac{\pi}{2},\; \frac{3\pi}{2},\; \frac{5\pi}{2},\; \frac{7\pi}{2}$$

Step 4: Divide by 2 to get $\theta = \frac{u}{2}$.

$$\theta = \frac{\pi}{4},\; \frac{3\pi}{4},\; \frac{5\pi}{4},\; \frac{7\pi}{4}$$

Final Answer: $\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{4},\; \dfrac{3\pi}{4},\; \dfrac{5\pi}{4},\; \dfrac{7\pi}{4}$

Example 6 (Extended): Factoring — Solve $\cos\theta(2\sin\theta - 1) = 0$ for $0 \leq \theta < 2\pi$

Step 1: The equation is already factored. Set each factor equal to zero.

Factor 1: $\cos\theta = 0$

Factor 2: $2\sin\theta - 1 = 0 \implies \sin\theta = \frac{1}{2}$

Step 2: Solve Factor 1 — $\cos\theta = 0$.
Cosine equals zero at: $\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}$ and $\theta = \frac{3\pi}{2}$

Step 3: Solve Factor 2 — $\sin\theta = \frac{1}{2}$.
Reference angle $= \frac{\pi}{6}$; sine positive in QI and QII:
$\theta = \frac{\pi}{6}$ and $\theta = \frac{5\pi}{6}$

Step 4: Combine all solutions.

Final Answer: $\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{6},\; \dfrac{\pi}{2},\; \dfrac{5\pi}{6},\; \dfrac{3\pi}{2}$

Key insight: Never divide both sides by $\cos\theta$ — you would lose $\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}$ and $\frac{3\pi}{2}$ entirely. Always factor and set each factor to zero.

Summary of Key Concepts

  • Solving a trig equation always begins with isolating the trig function using algebra.
  • The reference angle gives the magnitude; the ASTC rule and the sign of the value determine the quadrants.
  • Most trig equations have two solutions in $[0, 2\pi)$. Always check both quadrants.
  • For reciprocal functions (sec, csc, cot), convert to the corresponding primary function first: $\sec\theta = k \Rightarrow \cos\theta = \frac{1}{k}$.
  • General solutions use $+ 2\pi n$ for sine and cosine, and $+ \pi n$ for tangent and cotangent.
  • Quadratic-type equations — take the square root (with $\pm$) or factor, then solve each case separately.
  • For double-angle equations like $\cos(2\theta) = k$, let $u = 2\theta$, solve in the expanded interval $[0, 4\pi)$, then divide answers by 2.
  • Factored equations — set each factor equal to zero separately. Never divide both sides by a trig function.
  • Always leave answers in exact radian form (fractions of $\pi$), not decimal approximations or degrees.

14. $\sin\theta + \cos\theta = 0 \Rightarrow \sin\theta = -\cos\theta \Rightarrow \tan\theta = -1$

15. The period of a function is how long it takes to complete one full cycle. On the unit circle, sine and cosine complete one full cycle as $\theta$ goes from $0$ to $2\pi$ — one full revolution. So their general solutions add $2\pi n$. Tangent, however, completes a full cycle in only half a revolution: $\tan\theta$ goes from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$ and repeats between $0$ and $\pi$. This is because tangent is the ratio $\frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}$, and that ratio returns to the same value after rotating only halfway around the circle. So its general solution adds $\pi n$, capturing solutions every $\pi$ units rather than every $2\pi$ units.

16. The student divided both sides by $\cos\theta$. This is illegal when $\cos\theta$ could equal zero — dividing by zero is undefined and erases valid solutions. The values $\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}$ and $\theta = \frac{3\pi}{2}$ satisfy $\cos\theta = 0$, making the first factor of the original equation equal to zero. The correct approach is to set each factor equal to zero separately:

$\cos\theta = 0$ gives $\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}$ and $\frac{3\pi}{2}$; and $\sin\theta = \frac{1}{2}$ gives $\theta = \frac{\pi}{6}$ and $\frac{5\pi}{6}$.

Complete solution: $\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{6},\; \dfrac{\pi}{2},\; \dfrac{5\pi}{6},\; \dfrac{3\pi}{2}$
