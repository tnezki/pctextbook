10.D Logarithmic Equations with Extraneous Solutions

Logarithmic equations often produce multiple solutions when solved algebraically, but not all of those solutions are valid.
Understanding domain restrictions and checking solutions is essential to identifying extraneous solutions.
“Not every solution is a valid solution — always check.”

Learning Targets

I can identify extraneous solutions.

Introduction

When solving logarithmic equations, algebraic steps such as combining expressions or rewriting equations can introduce solutions that do not satisfy the original equation.

Because logarithms are only defined for positive values, every solution must be checked against domain restrictions.

Key Idea: Solving logarithmic equations requires checking all solutions for validity.

History

As logarithmic methods developed, mathematicians observed that algebraic manipulation could produce solutions that were not valid within the original context.

This led to the concept of extraneous solutions — results that must be tested and sometimes rejected.

Today, checking solutions is a standard part of solving logarithmic equations.

Key Concepts

Domain Restrictions

Logarithmic expressions are only defined when their arguments are positive.

$$ x > 0 $$

This restriction must be applied before solving any logarithmic equation.

Important: Values that make a logarithm undefined are not valid solutions.

Checking Solutions

After solving, all solutions must be checked in the original equation.

$$ \log(x-1) \Rightarrow x>1 $$

Any solution that violates this condition must be rejected.

Combining Logarithms

Logarithmic equations often require combining expressions before solving.

$$ \log x + \log(x-2) = \log 8 $$

Combine:

$$ \log[x(x-2)] = \log 8 $$ $$ x(x-2)=8 $$

Quadratic Structure

After combining logarithms, the resulting equation is often quadratic.

$$ x^2 - 2x - 8 = 0 $$

Solve using factoring or the quadratic formula.

$$ (x-4)(x+2)=0 $$

Extraneous Solutions

Some solutions obtained algebraically are not valid in the original equation.

$$ x=4,\ -2 $$

Check domain:

  • \(x=4\) is valid
  • \(x=-2\) is invalid
Key Idea: Extraneous solutions occur when algebra ignores domain restrictions.

Rational Logarithmic Equations

Subtracting logarithms creates rational expressions.

$$ \log(x-1) - \log(x-3) = \log 2 $$

Rewrite:

$$ \log\left(\frac{x-1}{x-3}\right)=\log 2 $$ $$ \frac{x-1}{x-3}=2 $$

Opposing Domains

Some equations create restrictions from both sides.

$$ \log(x-2)=\log(6-x) $$

Solve:

$$ x-2 = 6-x $$ $$ x=4 $$

Squared Arguments

Equations involving squared expressions can produce multiple solutions.

$$ \ln(x^2-4)=2 $$

Rewrite:

$$ x^2-4 = e^2 $$ $$ x = \pm\sqrt{e^2+4} $$

Validating All Solutions

Each solution must be checked against domain restrictions.

$$ x^2 - 4 > 0 $$

Only values that satisfy the inequality are valid.

Final Rule: Solve → Check → Reject invalid solutions.

Vocabulary

Extraneous Solution:

A solution obtained algebraically that does not satisfy the original equation.

Domain Restriction:

A condition that limits allowable values of a variable.

Quadratic Log Equation:

An equation that becomes quadratic after combining logarithmic expressions.

Valid Solution:

A solution that satisfies both the equation and all domain restrictions.

Opposing Domains:

Restrictions coming from both sides of an equation that limit possible solutions.

Strategies

Solving Logarithmic Equations:

  1. Identify domain restrictions first
  2. Combine logarithms using properties
  3. Rewrite as an exponential equation
  4. Solve the resulting equation

Checking Solutions:

  1. Substitute each solution into the original equation
  2. Check domain restrictions
  3. Reject any invalid solutions
Key Idea: Solve → Check → Reject invalid solutions.

Common Mistakes

  • Not checking solutions:
    Extraneous solutions must always be rejected.
  • Ignoring domain restrictions:
    Logarithmic expressions require positive arguments.
  • Incorrectly combining logs:
    Forgetting product or quotient rules leads to errors.
  • Algebra mistakes after combining:
    Errors occur when solving resulting quadratic equations.
  • Keeping invalid solutions:
    Solutions violating the domain must be removed.
  • Stopping too early:
    Solving is not complete until checking is done.

Summary

  • Logarithmic equations can produce multiple solutions.
  • Domain restrictions must always be applied.
  • Combining logs often leads to quadratic equations.
  • Extraneous solutions arise from algebraic manipulation.
  • All solutions must be checked.

Detailed Example Solutions

Example 3 — Quadratic Log Equation

$$ \log(x-1)+\log(x-4)=\log 6 $$ $$ \log[(x-1)(x-4)] = \log 6 $$ $$ (x-1)(x-4)=6 $$ $$ x^2 -5x -2 =0 $$ $$ x=\frac{5\pm\sqrt{33}}{2} $$

Check both solutions:

  • One satisfies domain → valid
  • One violates domain → extraneous

Example 4 — Rational Log Equation

$$ \log(x-1)-\log(x-3)=\log 2 $$ $$ \log\left(\frac{x-1}{x-3}\right)=\log 2 $$ $$ \frac{x-1}{x-3}=2 $$ $$ x-1=2x-6 $$ $$ x=5 $$

Check → valid

Example 5 — Opposing Domains

$$ \log(x-2)=\log(6-x) $$ $$ x-2=6-x $$ $$ x=4 $$

Check domain → valid

Example 6 — Squared Argument

$$ \ln(x^2-4)=2 $$ $$ x^2-4 = e^2 $$ $$ x = \pm\sqrt{e^2+4} $$

Check both → valid

Example 7 — Multi-Step with Extraneous Solution

$$ \log(x-2)+\log(x-3)=\log 4 $$ $$ \log[(x-2)(x-3)] = \log 4 $$ $$ (x-2)(x-3)=4 $$ $$ x^2-5x+2=0 $$ $$ x=\frac{5\pm\sqrt{17}}{2} $$

Check solutions:

  • One valid
  • One extraneous
