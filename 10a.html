10.A Properties of Logarithms

Logarithms allow us to rewrite complex exponential expressions into simpler forms by revealing the structure hidden inside multiplication and powers.
Instead of working directly with large expressions, logarithmic properties help break them apart or combine them strategically, making algebra more manageable and efficient.
“Logarithms reveal structure hidden inside exponents.”

Learning Targets

I can expand logarithmic expressions into simpler components.

I can use structure to simplify multi-step logarithmic expressions.

Introduction

Logarithms are the inverse of exponential functions. While exponentials involve repeated multiplication, logarithms allow us to express those relationships using addition and subtraction.

For example, an expression like \(x^3y^2\) represents multiplication and powers. Using logarithms, we can separate this structure into simpler pieces that are easier to analyze.

Key Idea: Logarithms transform multiplication into addition and exponents into multiplication.

This transformation makes logarithms a powerful tool for simplifying expressions and solving equations.

History

Logarithms were developed in the early 1600s by John Napier to simplify calculations involving large numbers. Before calculators, they allowed multiplication and division to be replaced with addition and subtraction.

Henry Briggs later refined logarithms into the base-10 system, making them more practical for scientific use. Natural logarithms, based on the constant \(e\), emerged through the study of growth and calculus.

Today, logarithms are essential in mathematics, science, and engineering wherever exponential relationships appear.

Key Concepts

Logarithms and Exponents

A logarithm answers the question: “What exponent produces a given value?”

$$ \log_b(x) = y \iff b^y = x $$

This inverse relationship connects logarithms directly to exponent rules.

Key Idea: Logarithm properties come from exponent rules.

Product Rule

Multiplication inside a logarithm becomes addition.

$$ \log(xy) = \log x + \log y $$

Example:

$$ \log(3x) = \log 3 + \log x $$

Quotient Rule

Division inside a logarithm becomes subtraction.

$$ \log\left(\frac{x}{y}\right) = \log x - \log y $$

Example:

$$ \log\left(\frac{5x}{y}\right) = \log 5 + \log x - \log y $$

Power Rule

Exponents inside a logarithm become coefficients.

$$ \log(x^n) = n\log x $$

Example:

$$ \log(x^4) = 4\log x $$

This also applies to roots and fractional exponents.

Example:

$$ \log(\sqrt{x}) = \frac{1}{2}\log x $$

Expanding Expressions

To expand, apply the product, quotient, and power rules step-by-step.

Example:

$$ \log\left(\frac{2x^3y}{z}\right) $$ $$ = \log 2 + 3\log x + \log y - \log z $$

Condensing Expressions

Condensing combines multiple logarithms into a single expression.

Example:

$$ 2\log x + \log y - \log z $$ $$ = \log(x^2) + \log y - \log z $$ $$ = \log\left(\frac{x^2y}{z}\right) $$

Restrictions on Logarithms

$$ x > 0 $$

The argument of a logarithm must always be positive.

Important: Logarithm properties do not apply to addition or subtraction.

Example:

$$ \log(x+y) \neq \log x + \log y $$

Vocabulary

Logarithm:

An expression that represents the exponent needed to produce a value.

Product Rule:

A property that rewrites multiplication inside a logarithm as addition.

Quotient Rule:

A property that rewrites division inside a logarithm as subtraction.

Power Rule:

A property that moves exponents in front of the logarithm as coefficients.

Expand:

Rewrite a logarithmic expression as a sum or difference of logarithms.

Condense:

Rewrite multiple logarithms as a single logarithmic expression.

Strategies

Expanding Logarithmic Expressions:

  1. Identify multiplication, division, and powers inside the logarithm
  2. Apply product and quotient rules first
  3. Apply the power rule last
  4. Expand completely

Condensing Logarithmic Expressions:

  1. Rewrite coefficients as exponents
  2. Combine using the product rule
  3. Apply the quotient rule if needed
  4. Write the final answer as a single logarithm
Key Idea: Expanding breaks expressions apart. Condensing builds them back together.

Common Mistakes

  • Trying to split addition inside a logarithm:
    $$ \log(x+y) \neq \log x + \log y $$
  • Forgetting to apply the power rule:
    Students often leave exponents inside instead of bringing them out.
  • Incorrect order of expansion:
    Apply product and quotient rules before the power rule.
  • Dropping coefficients when condensing:
    $$ 2\log x \neq \log x $$
  • Not rewriting coefficients as exponents:
    Must convert before combining logs.
  • Combining logs incorrectly:
    Multiplication and division only — not addition or subtraction.
  • Ignoring domain restrictions:
    Logarithms are only defined for positive arguments.

Summary

  • Logarithms are the inverse of exponential functions.
  • Product rule converts multiplication into addition.
  • Quotient rule converts division into subtraction.
  • Power rule converts exponents into coefficients.
  • Expanding breaks expressions apart into simpler terms.
  • Condensing combines expressions into a single logarithm.
  • Logarithm properties do not apply to addition or subtraction inside the argument.

Detailed Example Solutions

Example 1 — Product Rule

$$ \log(6x) $$

Apply the product rule:

$$ = \log 6 + \log x $$

Example 2 — Quotient Rule

$$ \log\left(\frac{x}{4}\right) $$

Apply the quotient rule:

$$ = \log x - \log 4 $$

Example 3 — Power Rule

$$ \log(x^5) $$

Apply the power rule:

$$ = 5\log x $$

Example 4 — Multiple Properties

$$ \log\left(\frac{3x^2y}{z}\right) $$

Apply rules step-by-step:

$$ = \log 3 + 2\log x + \log y - \log z $$

Example 5 — Condensing

$$ \log x + \log y $$

Apply product rule:

$$ = \log(xy) $$

Example 6 — Condensing with Powers

$$ 2\log x - \log y $$

Rewrite coefficient and combine:

$$ = \log(x^2) - \log y $$ $$ = \log\left(\frac{x^2}{y}\right) $$

Example 7 — Complex Expression

$$ 3\log x + \log y - 2\log z $$

Rewrite coefficients:

$$ = \log(x^3) + \log y - \log(z^2) $$

Combine:

$$ = \log\left(\frac{x^3y}{z^2}\right) $$
