SECTION 8.A

Factoring and Algebraic Structure

Multiplication builds expressions. Factoring reveals how they were built. When terms are combined, structure becomes hidden — but it does not disappear. Factoring is the process of uncovering that structure.
“Structure is not created by factoring — it is discovered.”

Learning Objectives

  • I can identify and factor the greatest common factor of a polynomial.
  • I can recognize and factor special product patterns.
  • I can factor trinomials with leading coefficient 1 and with leading coefficients greater than 1.
  • I can determine the most efficient factoring strategy based on expression structure.
  • I can justify that a polynomial is factored completely.

Introduction: Reversing Construction

When we expand $(x + 4)(x - 3)$, we combine two structured expressions into a single polynomial. The final result hides the multiplication that created it.

Factoring reverses that process. Instead of combining parts, we separate a polynomial back into expressions that multiply to produce it.

This is not guesswork. It is classification. Every polynomial has structural clues:

  • Common numerical factors
  • Repeated variable factors
  • Recognizable patterns like $a^2 - b^2$
  • Three-term structure suggesting a trinomial

Strong algebra students do not begin factoring immediately. They pause, observe, and classify.

Core Principle: Always factor the greatest common factor first. Then analyze the remaining structure.

Why Factoring Matters

Factoring connects directly to solving equations. If

$$ (x - 5)(x + 2) = 0 $$

then one factor must equal zero. That produces the solutions $x = 5$ or $x = -2$.

In graphing, these values become the zeros of the function — the points where the graph intersects the x-axis.

In higher mathematics, factoring is essential in calculus for simplifying rational expressions and analyzing limits.

Key Vocabulary and Structural Patterns

Factor: An expression that multiplies with another expression to produce a product.
Greatest Common Factor (GCF): The largest expression that divides evenly into all terms of a polynomial.
Difference of Squares: $$ a^2 - b^2 = (a - b)(a + b) $$
Sum of Cubes: $$ a^3 + b^3 = (a + b)(a^2 - ab + b^2) $$
Difference of Cubes: $$ a^3 - b^3 = (a - b)(a^2 + ab + b^2) $$

Strategic Factoring Order

Factoring should follow a consistent decision structure:

  1. Rewrite in descending powers.
  2. Factor out the GCF.
  3. Count the number of terms.
  4. Check for special product patterns.
  5. Factor trinomials or apply grouping if needed.
  6. Factor completely.
Factoring is efficient when it is systematic. Skipping structural analysis leads to unnecessary work and common errors.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Factoring Out a Greatest Common Factor

Factor:

$$ 15x^3 - 20x^2 $$

Step 1: Identify the greatest common factor.

The numerical GCF of 15 and 20 is 5. The variable GCF of $x^3$ and $x^2$ is $x^2$.

Step 2: Factor it out.

$$ 15x^3 - 20x^2 = 5x^2(3x - 4) $$

Final Answer: $5x^2(3x - 4)$

Always check whether the remaining expression can be factored further. Here, $3x - 4$ cannot.

Example 2: Factoring a Trinomial (Leading Coefficient 1)

Factor:

$$ x^2 + 9x + 14 $$

Step 1: Identify two numbers that multiply to 14 and add to 9.

The numbers are 7 and 2.

Step 2: Write the factors.

$$ x^2 + 9x + 14 = (x + 7)(x + 2) $$

Verification:

$$ (x + 7)(x + 2) = x^2 + 9x + 14 $$

Final Answer: $(x + 7)(x + 2)$

Example 3: Factoring a Trinomial (Leading Coefficient Greater Than 1)

Factor:

$$ 4x^2 + 11x + 6 $$

Step 1: Multiply $a \cdot c$.

$$ 4 \cdot 6 = 24 $$

Step 2: Find two numbers that multiply to 24 and add to 11.

The numbers are 8 and 3.

Step 3: Rewrite the middle term.

$$ 4x^2 + 8x + 3x + 6 $$

Step 4: Factor by grouping.

$$ 4x(x + 2) + 3(x + 2) $$ $$ (4x + 3)(x + 2) $$

Final Answer: $(4x + 3)(x + 2)$

Example 4: Difference of Squares

Factor:

$$ 25x^2 - 49 $$

Recognize the pattern:

$$ 25x^2 - 49 = (5x)^2 - 7^2 $$

Apply the identity:

$$ a^2 - b^2 = (a - b)(a + b) $$ $$ (5x - 7)(5x + 7) $$

Final Answer: $(5x - 7)(5x + 7)$

Example 5: Sum of Cubes

Factor:

$$ 8x^3 + 27 $$

Rewrite as perfect cubes:

$$ (2x)^3 + 3^3 $$

Apply the identity:

$$ a^3 + b^3 = (a + b)(a^2 - ab + b^2) $$ $$ (2x + 3)(4x^2 - 6x + 9) $$

Final Answer: $(2x + 3)(4x^2 - 6x + 9)$

Example 6: Multi-Stage Factoring

Factor completely:

$$ 12x^3 - 75x $$

Step 1: Factor the GCF.

$$ 3x(4x^2 - 25) $$

Step 2: Recognize difference of squares.

$$ 4x^2 - 25 = (2x)^2 - 5^2 $$ $$ (2x - 5)(2x + 5) $$

Final Answer:

$$ 3x(2x - 5)(2x + 5) $$

Notice: Factoring happened in stages. Stopping after Step 1 would not be complete.

Common Mistakes

  • Skipping the Greatest Common Factor:
    Beginning with trinomial factoring before removing the GCF often leads to incomplete answers and unnecessary complexity.
  • Stopping After One Step:
    Factoring may require multiple stages. Always check each factor to determine whether additional structure remains.
  • Incorrect Number Pair in Trinomials:
    Students sometimes find two numbers that multiply correctly but fail to verify that they add correctly. Both multiplication and addition conditions must be satisfied.
  • Misapplying Special Product Patterns:
    Difference of squares applies only to expressions of the form $a^2 - b^2$. It does not apply to sums, and it requires exactly two terms.
  • Sign Errors:
    Incorrect placement of negative signs changes the entire structure of the factorization. Always verify by multiplying the factors back together.
  • Forgetting to Factor Completely:
    An answer is not complete if any factor can still be simplified or factored further.

Summary of Factoring Strategy and Structure

Structural Cue What It Tells You Action to Take
All terms share a factor A greatest common factor exists. Factor out the GCF first.
Two terms, both perfect squares, subtraction Difference of squares pattern. Apply $(a-b)(a+b)$.
Two perfect cubes Sum or difference of cubes pattern. Apply cube identity carefully (binomial × trinomial).
Three terms (trinomial) Possible quadratic structure. Find two numbers that multiply to $ac$ and add to $b$.
Four terms Possible grouping structure. Factor by grouping and look for common binomials.

Core Takeaways:
  • Factoring is structural classification, not memorized steps.
  • Always remove the GCF before applying other methods.
  • Recognize patterns before performing algebraic manipulation.
  • Verify your result by multiplying the factors.
  • An expression is factored completely when no factor can be simplified further.

Solutions

Level 1 — Structural Recognition

1. $$ 18x^2 - 27x = 9x(2x - 3) $$

2. $$ x^2 + 13x + 36 = (x + 9)(x + 4) $$

3. $$ 49x^2 - 16 = (7x - 4)(7x + 4) $$

4. $$ x^3 - 125 = (x - 5)(x^2 + 5x + 25) $$

Level 2 — Multi-Step Factoring

5. $$ 9x^3 - 36x = 9x(x^2 - 4) = 9x(x - 2)(x + 2) $$

6. $$ 6x^2 + 17x + 12 $$ Multiply $a \cdot c = 72$ Numbers: 9 and 8 $$ 6x^2 + 9x + 8x + 12 $$ $$ 3x(2x + 3) + 4(2x + 3) $$ $$ (3x + 4)(2x + 3) $$

7. $$ 3x^2 - 48 = 3(x^2 - 16) = 3(x - 4)(x + 4) $$

8. $$ x^4 - 81 = (x^2)^2 - 9^2 $$ $$ (x^2 - 9)(x^2 + 9) $$ $$ (x - 3)(x + 3)(x^2 + 9) $$

Level 3 — Advanced Structure

9. Group: $$ (15x^3 + 10x^2) - (6x + 4) $$ $$ 5x^2(3x + 2) - 2(3x + 2) $$ $$ (5x^2 - 2)(3x + 2) $$

10. $$ 2x^3 - 50x = 2x(x^2 - 25) = 2x(x - 5)(x + 5) $$

11. Multiply $a \cdot c = -24$ Numbers: 6 and -4 $$ 8x^2 + 6x - 4x - 3 $$ $$ 2x(4x + 3) - 1(4x + 3) $$ $$ (2x - 1)(4x + 3) $$

12. $$ x^4 - 25x^2 = x^2(x^2 - 25) $$ $$ x^2(x - 5)(x + 5) $$

Level 4 — Challenge

13. Expand: $$ (3x - 2)(2x + 5) $$ $$ = 6x^2 + 15x - 4x - 10 $$ $$ = 6x^2 + 11x - 10 $$

14. One possible answer: $$ x^3 - 216 $$ $$ = (x - 6)(x^2 + 6x + 36) $$

15. One possible answer: $$ 4x^3 - 36x $$ Factor: $$ 4x(x^2 - 9) = 4x(x - 3)(x + 3) $$

Level 5 — Justify

16. Removing the GCF reduces the size of coefficients and simplifies later factoring, decreasing arithmetic errors and preventing incomplete results.

17. $x^2 + 4$ is a sum of squares. There are no real numbers whose square equals $-4$, so it cannot be factored over the real numbers.

18. The difference of squares works because: $$ (a - b)(a + b) = a^2 - b^2 $$ The middle terms cancel during expansion, leaving only the difference of the squares.
