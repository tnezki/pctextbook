SECTION 7.C

Half-Angle, Sum, and Difference Identities

The unit circle gives us exact values for a fixed set of special angles — but what about angles that fall between them? The sum, difference, and half-angle identities are the keys that unlock exact values for angles like 15°, 75°, and $\frac{\pi}{12}$ that the unit circle alone cannot provide. By combining or splitting angles we already know, we can reach angles that would otherwise require a calculator — and still get a perfectly exact answer.
“ You don't need to know every angle — you just need to know how to break any angle into ones you do know. ”

Learning Objectives

  • I can apply half-angle formulas for sine and cosine to evaluate exact expressions.
  • I can apply the half-angle formula for tangent and determine the appropriate sign.
  • I can apply angle sum and difference identities to rewrite and evaluate trigonometric expressions.
  • I can justify multi-step reasoning using algebraic structure.

Introduction: Reaching Beyond the Unit Circle

Think back to the unit circle. You have memorized exact coordinates for angles like $\frac{\pi}{6}$, $\frac{\pi}{4}$, and $\frac{\pi}{3}$. But what if someone asks you for $\cos(75°)$? That angle is not on the standard unit circle. A calculator gives you a decimal approximation — but an exact answer requires a new tool.

The key insight is that $75° = 45° + 30°$. Both $45°$ and $30°$ are special angles with known exact values. If we had a formula for $\cos(A + B)$ in terms of $\cos A$, $\cos B$, $\sin A$, and $\sin B$, we could compute $\cos(75°)$ exactly — and we do. That is precisely what the sum and difference identities provide.

Similarly, the half-angle identities let you go the other direction. If you know $\cos(\theta)$, you can find exact values for $\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)$ and $\cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)$. For example, since $\frac{\pi}{4}$ is a known angle, we can use the half-angle formula to find $\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{8}\right)$ exactly.

One important feature of half-angle formulas is the $\pm$ sign. The formula produces a square root, and a square root always has two possible values — positive and negative. The correct sign is determined by the quadrant of $\frac{\theta}{2}$, not the quadrant of $\theta$ itself. This is a critical detail that trips up many students.

📚 Historical Note: The sum and difference formulas for sine and cosine appear in Ptolemy's Almagest (c. 150 AD) in a geometric form called Ptolemy's theorem for cyclic quadrilaterals. The algebraic form we use today was developed by François Viète (1540–1603), a French mathematician who pioneered the use of letters to represent unknowns — an approach we now take for granted. The half-angle identities follow directly from the double-angle cosine formulas established in Section 7.B.
🔗 Connections to Other Subjects and Real Life:

Navigation and Surveying: Before GPS, surveyors used exact trig values to compute distances and bearings. A surveyor measuring an angle of 75° between two landmarks could use the sum identity to get an exact result without a calculator.

Physics — Wave Interference: When two sound waves or light waves meet, the resulting amplitude involves $\sin(A + B)$ and $\cos(A + B)$. The sum and difference identities are used to simplify the combined wave equation and predict whether the waves reinforce (constructive interference) or cancel (destructive interference).

Music Theory: Chords are combinations of frequencies. The sum identity $\sin A \cos B = \frac{1}{2}[\sin(A+B) + \sin(A-B)]$ describes how two pure tones combine to produce new frequencies — the mathematical basis of harmony and overtones.

Calculus: The sum-to-product identities derived from sum/difference formulas are essential for evaluating certain integrals and derivatives involving products of sine and cosine functions.

Key Vocabulary and Concepts

Half-Angle Identity: A formula that expresses the sine, cosine, or tangent of $\frac{\theta}{2}$ in terms of the trig functions of $\theta$ (the full angle). The formulas contain square roots and therefore require a $\pm$ sign whose value depends on which quadrant $\frac{\theta}{2}$ lies in.
Sum Identity: A formula expressing $\sin(A + B)$ or $\cos(A + B)$ in terms of the sines and cosines of $A$ and $B$ separately. These allow you to evaluate trig functions of non-standard angles by writing them as sums of two special angles.
Difference Identity: A formula expressing $\sin(A - B)$ or $\cos(A - B)$. The structure is identical to the sum identity except that one sign is flipped — but which sign flips is different for sine and cosine, so careful attention is required.
Sign Determination: When applying a half-angle formula, the $\pm$ sign is determined by the quadrant of $\frac{\theta}{2}$, not the quadrant of $\theta$. Always determine which quadrant $\frac{\theta}{2}$ lies in before choosing the sign.

The Complete Identity Reference

Sum and Difference Identities

$$\sin(A + B) = \sin A \cos B + \cos A \sin B$$ $$\sin(A - B) = \sin A \cos B - \cos A \sin B$$ $$\cos(A + B) = \cos A \cos B - \sin A \sin B$$ $$\cos(A - B) = \cos A \cos B + \sin A \sin B$$

Half-Angle Identities

$$\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) = \pm\sqrt{\frac{1 - \cos\theta}{2}}$$ $$\cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) = \pm\sqrt{\frac{1 + \cos\theta}{2}}$$ $$\tan\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) = \frac{\sin\theta}{1 + \cos\theta} = \frac{1 - \cos\theta}{\sin\theta}$$

The Sign Pattern for Sine and Cosine Identities

A common point of confusion: in the sum/difference formulas, cosine and sine handle signs differently.

How to Determine the Sign in Half-Angle Formulas

✓ Three-Step Sign Determination Process:
  1. Find the value of $\theta$ from the given information.
  2. Compute $\frac{\theta}{2}$ — this is the actual angle whose trig value you want.
  3. Identify which quadrant $\frac{\theta}{2}$ lies in. Then apply the ASTC rule to determine the sign of sine or cosine in that quadrant.

⚠ Common Mistakes to Avoid

Mistake 1 — Using the quadrant of $\theta$ instead of $\frac{\theta}{2}$ for sign determination: The $\pm$ in $\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) = \pm\sqrt{\frac{1-\cos\theta}{2}}$ depends on the quadrant of $\frac{\theta}{2}$, not $\theta$. For example, if $\theta = \frac{3\pi}{2}$ (Quadrant III/IV boundary), then $\frac{\theta}{2} = \frac{3\pi}{4}$ which is in Quadrant II — always find $\frac{\theta}{2}$ first.


Mistake 2 — Flipping the wrong sign in cosine sum/difference: In $\cos(A + B) = \cos A \cos B \mathbf{-} \sin A \sin B$, the sign is minus for a sum. In $\cos(A - B) = \cos A \cos B \mathbf{+} \sin A \sin B$, the sign is plus for a difference. This is the opposite of what most students expect. Use the memory tip: cosine flips the sign.


Mistake 3 — Forgetting to fully simplify radicals: After applying a half-angle formula, the result often contains nested radicals or unsimplified fractions. Always rationalize and simplify. For example, $\sqrt{\frac{2 - \sqrt{3}}{4}} = \frac{\sqrt{2 - \sqrt{3}}}{2}$, not just $\sqrt{\frac{2-\sqrt{3}}{4}}$.


Mistake 4 — Using sum identity for a single angle: If you need $\sin(75°)$, you must split it as $\sin(45° + 30°)$ and apply the full identity. You cannot write $\sin(75°) = \sin(45°) + \sin(30°)$. Trig functions do NOT distribute over addition.


Mistake 5 — Mixing up which formula to use: Half-angle formulas are for going from $\theta$ to $\frac{\theta}{2}$. Sum/difference formulas are for combining two separate angles. Do not apply a half-angle formula when you actually need a sum identity, or vice versa.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Half-Angle — Evaluate $\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi}{8}\right)$ Exactly

Find the exact value of $\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi}{8}\right)$ using the half-angle identity.

Step 1: Recognize $\frac{\pi}{8}$ as half of a known angle.
$\frac{\pi}{8} = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{\pi}{4}$, so we use $\theta = \frac{\pi}{4}$.

Step 2: Determine the sign.
$\frac{\theta}{2} = \frac{\pi}{8}$ is between $0$ and $\frac{\pi}{2}$ — Quadrant I — so sine is positive.

Step 3: Apply the half-angle formula for sine.

$$\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{8}\right) = \sqrt{\frac{1 - \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)}{2}} = \sqrt{\frac{1 - \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}}{2}}$$

Step 4: Simplify the fraction inside the radical.

$$= \sqrt{\frac{\frac{2 - \sqrt{2}}{2}}{2}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 - \sqrt{2}}{4}} = \frac{\sqrt{2 - \sqrt{2}}}{2}$$

Final Answer: $\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi}{8}\right) = \dfrac{\sqrt{2 - \sqrt{2}}}{2}$

Example 2: Sum Identity — Evaluate $\cos(75°)$ Exactly

Find the exact value of $\cos(75°)$.

Step 1: Write $75°$ as a sum of two special angles.
$75° = 45° + 30°$

Step 2: Apply $\cos(A + B) = \cos A \cos B - \sin A \sin B$.

$$\cos(75°) = \cos(45°)\cos(30°) - \sin(45°)\sin(30°)$$

Step 3: Substitute exact values from the unit circle.

$$= \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} - \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{2}$$

Step 4: Multiply and combine.

$$= \frac{\sqrt{6}}{4} - \frac{\sqrt{2}}{4} = \frac{\sqrt{6} - \sqrt{2}}{4}$$

Final Answer: $\cos(75°) = \dfrac{\sqrt{6} - \sqrt{2}}{4}$

Example 3: Sum Identity — Expand $\sin(45° + 30°)$

Apply the sum identity to rewrite $\sin(45° + 30°)$ and evaluate exactly.

Step 1: Apply $\sin(A + B) = \sin A \cos B + \cos A \sin B$.

$$\sin(45° + 30°) = \sin(45°)\cos(30°) + \cos(45°)\sin(30°)$$

Step 2: Substitute unit circle values.

$$= \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} + \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{2}$$

Step 3: Multiply and combine.

$$= \frac{\sqrt{6}}{4} + \frac{\sqrt{2}}{4} = \frac{\sqrt{6} + \sqrt{2}}{4}$$

Final Answer: $\sin(75°) = \dfrac{\sqrt{6} + \sqrt{2}}{4}$

Note: We can verify — $75°$ is in Quadrant I, so sine should be positive. ✓

Example 4: Simplify $\cos(A - B) + \cos(A + B)$

Expand and simplify $\cos(A - B) + \cos(A + B)$ completely.

Step 1: Expand each term using the sum and difference identities.

$$\cos(A - B) = \cos A \cos B + \sin A \sin B$$ $$\cos(A + B) = \cos A \cos B - \sin A \sin B$$

Step 2: Add the two expressions.

$$\cos(A-B) + \cos(A+B) = (\cos A \cos B + \sin A \sin B) + (\cos A \cos B - \sin A \sin B)$$

Step 3: Combine like terms.
The $\sin A \sin B$ terms cancel:

$$= 2\cos A \cos B$$

Final Answer: $\cos(A - B) + \cos(A + B) = 2\cos A \cos B$

This result is called a product-to-sum identity and appears in signal processing and physics.

Example 5 (Extended): Half-Angle with Given Value — Find $\sin\left(\dfrac{\theta}{2}\right)$

Given that $\cos\theta = \dfrac{1}{3}$ and $\theta$ is in Quadrant I, find $\tan\left(\dfrac{\theta}{2}\right)$.

Step 1: Determine the quadrant of $\frac{\theta}{2}$.
Since $\theta$ is in Quadrant I, $0 < \theta < \frac{\pi}{2}$, so $0 < \frac{\theta}{2} < \frac{\pi}{4}$. Therefore $\frac{\theta}{2}$ is also in Quadrant I.

Step 2: Use the sign-free tangent half-angle formula.
The tangent form $\tan\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) = \frac{\sin\theta}{1 + \cos\theta}$ requires no $\pm$ sign — it automatically gives the correct sign. We need $\sin\theta$ first.

Step 3: Find $\sin\theta$ using the Pythagorean identity.

$$\sin^2\theta = 1 - \left(\frac{1}{3}\right)^2 = 1 - \frac{1}{9} = \frac{8}{9}$$ $$\sin\theta = \frac{\sqrt{8}}{3} = \frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3} \quad \text{(positive, QI)}$$

Step 4: Apply the tangent half-angle formula.

$$\tan\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) = \frac{\sin\theta}{1 + \cos\theta} = \frac{\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3}}{1 + \frac{1}{3}} = \frac{\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3}}{\frac{4}{3}} = \frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3} \cdot \frac{3}{4} = \frac{2\sqrt{2}}{4} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$$

Final Answer: $\tan\left(\dfrac{\theta}{2}\right) = \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$

Example 6 (Extended): Harder Evaluation — $\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi}{12}\right)$

Evaluate $\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi}{12}\right)$ exactly using a difference identity.

Step 1: Write $\frac{\pi}{12}$ as a difference of two known angles.
$\frac{\pi}{12} = \frac{\pi}{4} - \frac{\pi}{6}$   (since $\frac{3\pi}{12} - \frac{2\pi}{12} = \frac{\pi}{12}$)

Step 2: Apply $\sin(A - B) = \sin A \cos B - \cos A \sin B$.

$$\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{12}\right) = \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{6}\right) - \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{6}\right)$$

Step 3: Substitute exact values.

$$= \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} - \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \frac{\sqrt{6}}{4} - \frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}$$

Final Answer: $\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi}{12}\right) = \dfrac{\sqrt{6} - \sqrt{2}}{4}$

Note: $\frac{\pi}{12} = 15°$ is in Quadrant I, so sine must be positive. Since $\sqrt{6} > \sqrt{2}$, the answer is indeed positive. ✓

Summary of Key Concepts

  • Sum and difference identities allow you to evaluate trig functions of angles that are not on the standard unit circle by decomposing them into sums or differences of known angles.
  • $\sin(A \pm B) = \sin A \cos B \pm \cos A \sin B$ — the sign in sine stays the same.
  • $\cos(A \pm B) = \cos A \cos B \mp \sin A \sin B$ — the sign in cosine flips.
  • Trig functions do not distribute: $\sin(A + B) \neq \sin A + \sin B$. Always use the identity.
  • Half-angle formulas express $\sin\frac{\theta}{2}$, $\cos\frac{\theta}{2}$, and $\tan\frac{\theta}{2}$ in terms of $\cos\theta$.
  • The $\pm$ in sine and cosine half-angle formulas is determined by the quadrant of $\frac{\theta}{2}$, not $\theta$.
  • The tangent half-angle formula $\tan\frac{\theta}{2} = \frac{\sin\theta}{1+\cos\theta}$ requires no $\pm$ and is often easier to use.
  • Adding $\cos(A+B) + \cos(A-B)$ produces $2\cos A \cos B$ — the sine terms cancel. This is the product-to-sum identity for cosine.
  • When simplifying after substitution, always rationalize denominators and simplify radicals completely.
