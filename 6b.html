SECTION 6.B
Sketch Graphs of Polynomial Functions in Factored Form

Learning Objectives

I can sketch the graph of a polynomial function from its factored form, using roots to determine key features and behavior.

I can create and interpret sign charts to analyze where polynomial functions are positive and negative, connecting structure to behavior.

I can look for and use the structure of factored form to make predictions about the shape and key features of polynomial graphs.

Introduction: Reading Graphs from Factors

The factored form of a polynomial is like a blueprint for its graph. Each factor tells you where the graph intersects the x-axis, and how the polynomial behaves at that intersection. By understanding this structure, you can sketch a polynomial's shape without relying on a calculator or plotting many points. This skill connects algebra to geometry in a powerful way.

Real-World Connection

Engineers designing roller coasters use polynomial functions to create smooth curves. The factored form helps them identify critical points where the track changes direction or touches a boundary. Scientists studying population dynamics use polynomials to model how populations rise and fall. Understanding how factors determine the graph's shape helps in both designing and analyzing real-world systems.

Historical Note

The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra, proven by Carl Friedrich Gauss in 1799, states that every polynomial of degree $n$ has exactly $n$ roots (counting multiplicity). This theorem connects the algebraic factors to the visual behavior of the graph.

Key Vocabulary and Concepts

Root (or Zero): A value of $x$ where the polynomial equals zero, $f(x) = 0$. Graphically, roots are where the graph crosses or touches the x-axis.

Multiplicity: The number of times a factor appears in the polynomial. If $(x - 2)$ appears twice, the root $x = 2$ has multiplicity 2.

How Multiplicity Affects the Graph

When a root has an odd multiplicity, the graph crosses the x-axis at that point. When a root has an even multiplicity, the graph touches the x-axis but bounces back without crossing.

  • Multiplicity 1: The graph crosses the x-axis sharply.
  • Multiplicity 2: The graph touches the x-axis and bounces back.
  • Multiplicity 3 or higher: The graph crosses with a flatter appearance.

Understanding Factored Form

A polynomial in factored form looks like: $f(x) = a(x - r_1)(x - r_2)(x - r_3)\cdots$

The value of $a$ determines whether the polynomial eventually goes up or down as $x$ increases to infinity.

Examples

Example 1

Sketch the graph of $f(x) = (x - 1)(x + 2)(x - 4)$ using the roots.

Example 2

Sketch $f(x) = (x - 2)^2(x + 1)$ and explain how the multiplicity affects the graph.

Example 3

For $f(x) = -(x + 3)(x - 1)^2(x - 5)$, determine the roots, their multiplicities, and the end behavior.

Example 4

Sketch $f(x) = 2(x + 2)(x - 3)$ and find the y-intercept.

Summary of Key Concepts

  • Factored form directly reveals the roots (x-intercepts).
  • A root's multiplicity tells you how the graph behaves at that point: odd multiplicity means crossing, even multiplicity means bouncing.
  • The leading coefficient determines end behavior.
  • The degree determines whether ends go same or opposite directions.
  • The y-intercept is found by setting $x = 0$.
  • By combining information about roots, multiplicity, and end behavior, you can sketch an accurate graph without plotting many points.

Practice Problems

Easy

1. Identify the roots and multiplicities for $f(x) = (x - 2)(x + 5)$.
2. For $f(x) = (x + 1)^2(x - 3)$, identify even and odd multiplicities.
3. Identify the degree and leading coefficient of $f(x) = -2(x - 1)(x + 3)$.
4. Determine whether $f(x) = (x - 4)^3$ crosses or bounces at its root.

Medium

5. Find the y-intercept of $f(x) = 2(x - 3)(x + 1)$.
6. Describe the end behavior of $f(x) = -3(x + 2)(x - 1)(x - 4)$.
7. Identify the roots and sketch $f(x) = (x - 2)^2(x + 1)$.
8. Identify the roots and sketch $f(x) = -2x^2(x - 3)$.

Hard

9. Sketch $f(x) = (x + 3)(x - 1)^2(x - 5)$.
10. Construct a polynomial with roots at -2 (multiplicity 2) and 3 (multiplicity 1).
11. Construct a polynomial of degree 4 with two simple roots and one double root.
12. A polynomial $f(x) = a(x + 3)x^2(x - 2)$ has y-intercept 12. Find $a$.

Challenge

13. Write a polynomial with one even multiplicity root and two odd multiplicity roots.
14. A polynomial has odd degree and negative leading coefficient. Describe its end behavior.
15. Explain how multiplicity affects the local shape of a graph.

Justify

16. Explain why even multiplicity roots do not change the sign of a function.
17. Justify how the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra relates to the number of x-intercepts.
18. Explain how factored form allows you to sketch a graph without plotting many points.

Solutions

Easy

1. $f(x) = (x - 2)(x + 5)$ Roots: $x = 2$ and $x = -5$ Both have multiplicity 1 (each factor appears once), so the graph crosses the x-axis at both roots.

2. $f(x) = (x + 1)^2(x - 3)$ Root at $x = -1$ has multiplicity 2 (even). Root at $x = 3$ has multiplicity 1 (odd). The graph bounces at $x = -1$ and crosses at $x = 3$.

3. $f(x) = -2(x - 1)(x + 3)$ Degree = 2 (two linear factors). Leading coefficient = $-2$. The polynomial has even degree and negative leading coefficient.

4. $f(x) = (x - 4)^3$ Root: $x = 4$ with multiplicity 3 (odd). The graph crosses the x-axis at $x = 4$, but with a flatter appearance than multiplicity 1.

Medium

5. $f(x) = 2(x - 3)(x + 1)$ Find the y-intercept by setting $x = 0$: \[ f(0) = 2(0 - 3)(0 + 1) = 2(-3)(1) = -6 \] Y-intercept: $(0, -6)$

6. $f(x) = -3(x + 2)(x - 1)(x - 4)$ Degree = 3 (odd). Leading coefficient = $-3$ (negative). End behavior: As $x \to -\infty$, $f(x) \to +\infty$ (up on the left). As $x \to +\infty$, $f(x) \to -\infty$ (down on the right).

7. $f(x) = (x - 2)^2(x + 1)$ Roots: $x = 2$ (multiplicity 2), $x = -1$ (multiplicity 1). The graph crosses at $x = -1$ and bounces at $x = 2$. Degree = 3 (odd), leading coefficient positive. End behavior: down on left, up on right.

8. $f(x) = -2x^2(x - 3)$ Roots: $x = 0$ (multiplicity 2), $x = 3$ (multiplicity 1). The graph bounces at $x = 0$ and crosses at $x = 3$. Degree = 3 (odd), leading coefficient negative. End behavior: up on left, down on right.

Hard

9. $f(x) = (x + 3)(x - 1)^2(x - 5)$ Roots: $x = -3$ (1), $x = 1$ (2), $x = 5$ (1). Cross at $x = -3$ and $x = 5$. Bounce at $x = 1$. Degree = 4 (even), leading coefficient positive. End behavior: up on both ends.

10. A polynomial with roots at $x = -2$ (multiplicity 2) and $x = 3$ (multiplicity 1): \[ f(x) = a(x + 2)^2(x - 3) \] If no leading coefficient specified, choose $a = 1$: \[ f(x) = (x + 2)^2(x - 3) \]

11. Example: \[ f(x) = (x - 1)(x + 2)(x - 3)^2 \] Roots: $1$, $-2$, and $3$ (multiplicity 2). Degree = 4. One double root and two simple roots.

12. $f(x) = a(x + 3)x^2(x - 2)$ Y-intercept occurs at $x = 0$. \[ f(0) = a(3)(0)^2(-2) = 0 \] Since $x^2$ is a factor, the y-intercept must be 0. Therefore, no value of $a$ can make the y-intercept equal to 12.

Challenge

13. Example: \[ f(x) = (x - 1)^2(x + 3)(x - 4) \] Root at $x = 1$ has even multiplicity. Roots at $x = -3$ and $x = 4$ have odd multiplicity.

14. If degree is odd and leading coefficient is negative: As $x \to -\infty$, $f(x) \to +\infty$. As $x \to +\infty$, $f(x) \to -\infty$. The graph rises on the left and falls on the right.

15. Multiplicity affects local behavior at a root: Odd multiplicity → graph crosses the axis. Even multiplicity → graph touches and bounces. Higher multiplicity produces a flatter shape near the root.

Justify

16. An even multiplicity root does not change the sign of the function because the factor is squared (or raised to an even power). The value remains positive on both sides of the root, so the graph does not cross the axis.

17. The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra states that a polynomial of degree $n$ has exactly $n$ roots (counting multiplicity). This explains why the number of x-intercepts corresponds to the total multiplicities of the factors.

18. Factored form reveals the roots immediately. Knowing the roots, multiplicities, degree, and leading coefficient allows you to determine intercepts and end behavior, making it possible to sketch the graph without plotting many points.
