SECTION 7.A

Exact Trigonometric Values

The unit circle is one of the most powerful tools in all of mathematics — a simple circle with radius one that encodes the exact values of sine, cosine, and tangent for every special angle you will ever need. Rather than reaching for a calculator, you will learn to read the circle like a map, using patterns and symmetry to find answers with perfect precision. This section builds the foundation upon which all future trigonometry depends.
“ The circle has no beginning and no end — and neither does the knowledge you gain from understanding it. ”

Learning Objectives

  • I can determine exact values of sine, cosine, and tangent for special angles on the unit circle.
  • I can determine exact values of all six trigonometric functions in Quadrants II–IV using reference angles.
  • I can interpret exact trigonometric values graphically in radians, including multiples of $\pi$.
  • I can evaluate trig functions for negative angles and angles greater than $2\pi$ using coterminal reasoning.

Introduction: The Language of Circles

Long before calculators existed, mathematicians needed exact answers — not approximations. An engineer designing a bridge or an astronomer predicting a planet's position could not afford rounding errors that compound over time. They needed to know, without any doubt, that the sine of 30° is exactly $\frac{1}{2}$ — not 0.4999 or 0.5001, but $\frac{1}{2}$. The unit circle makes this possible.

The unit circle is a circle centered at the origin with radius 1. Every point on this circle has coordinates $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$, where $\theta$ is the angle measured counterclockwise from the positive x-axis. This elegant connection between a circle and trigonometry means that finding a trig value is as simple as identifying a coordinate on the circle.

What makes this especially powerful is that you do not need to memorize all 16 special angles around the circle. You only need three coordinate pairs from Quadrant I. From there, the symmetry of the circle does the rest — the same values repeat in every quadrant, with only their signs changing based on where you are.

This section also extends your reach. What if the angle is negative, or greater than $2\pi$? The concept of coterminal angles — angles that land on the same point on the circle — lets you reduce any angle, no matter how large or strange, down to a familiar reference.

📚 Historical Note: The systematic study of the relationship between angles and coordinates dates back to Hipparchus of Nicaea (c. 190–120 BC), who is often called the father of trigonometry. He created the first known trigonometric table to help predict astronomical events. Indian mathematicians of the 5th century AD, particularly Aryabhata, refined these ideas into the sine function we use today. The unit circle formulation we use in modern classrooms was fully developed by Leonhard Euler in the 18th century.
🔗 Connections to Other Subjects and Real Life:

Physics: Simple harmonic motion — the back-and-forth swing of a pendulum or the oscillation of a spring — is described entirely using sine and cosine. Exact values let physicists write clean equations without decimal clutter.

Engineering & Architecture: Roof pitch angles, structural load calculations, and signal processing all rely on exact trig values. A civil engineer computing the forces on a bridge cable at exactly 30° to the horizontal uses $\frac{1}{2}$, not a rounded decimal.

Music and Sound: Sound waves are sine waves. The frequencies of musical notes correspond to exact mathematical ratios. Audio engineers use trig to design speaker systems and equalizers.

Computer Graphics: Every time a character rotates in a video game, the game engine is computing sine and cosine values to move vertices around a circle. Exact values for common angles make these computations faster and more reliable.

Key Vocabulary and Concepts

Unit Circle: A circle centered at the origin $(0,0)$ with radius $r = 1$. Every point on the circle has coordinates $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$, where $\theta$ is the angle formed with the positive x-axis. Because the radius is 1, the x-coordinate is the cosine and the y-coordinate is the sine — no extra computation needed.
Reference Angle ($\theta'$): The positive acute angle between the terminal side of $\theta$ and the nearest part of the x-axis. Reference angles are always between $0$ and $\frac{\pi}{2}$. The trig values of $\theta$ have the same absolute value as the trig values of $\theta'$ — only the sign may change depending on the quadrant.
Coterminal Angles: Two angles are coterminal if their terminal sides land on the same point on the unit circle. You can find a coterminal angle by adding or subtracting any multiple of $2\pi$ (one full revolution): $$\theta_{\text{coterminal}} = \theta \pm 2\pi k, \quad k \in \mathbb{Z}$$
Special Angles: The angles $0,\ \frac{\pi}{6},\ \frac{\pi}{4},\ \frac{\pi}{3},\ \frac{\pi}{2}$ (and their multiples) are called special angles because their exact trig values can be written using simple fractions and radicals. All other angles require a calculator.
Reciprocal Trig Functions: The three primary functions — sine, cosine, and tangent — each have a reciprocal: $$\csc\theta = \frac{1}{\sin\theta}, \quad \sec\theta = \frac{1}{\cos\theta}, \quad \cot\theta = \frac{1}{\tan\theta}$$

The Three Quadrant-I Coordinate Pairs to Memorize

In Quadrant I, as the angle increases from $0$ to $\frac{\pi}{2}$, cosine decreases and sine increases. Memorize these three pairs; everything else follows from symmetry:

$$\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2},\ \frac{1}{2}\right) \text{ at } \frac{\pi}{6} \qquad \left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2},\ \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right) \text{ at } \frac{\pi}{4} \qquad \left(\frac{1}{2},\ \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right) \text{ at } \frac{\pi}{3}$$

Memory trick: The numerators for sine at these three angles are $\sqrt{1},\ \sqrt{2},\ \sqrt{3}$ — all divided by 2. Cosine runs in the reverse order.

⚠ Common Mistakes to Avoid

Mistake 1 — Swapping sine and cosine: Remember, cosine is the x-coordinate and sine is the y-coordinate. Students often reverse these, especially under pressure. The phrase "x comes before y, cosine comes before sine" can help.


Mistake 2 — Forgetting the sign from the quadrant: The reference angle gives you the magnitude, but you must still apply the correct sign from the ASTC rule. Example: the reference angle for $\frac{5\pi}{6}$ is $\frac{\pi}{6}$, so $|\sin\left(\frac{5\pi}{6}\right)| = \frac{1}{2}$, but since $\frac{5\pi}{6}$ is in Quadrant II where sine is positive, the answer is $+\frac{1}{2}$.


Mistake 3 — Incorrect coterminal reduction: When subtracting $2\pi$ from an angle, make sure to use a common denominator. For example, $\frac{9\pi}{4} - 2\pi = \frac{9\pi}{4} - \frac{8\pi}{4} = \frac{\pi}{4}$, not $\frac{9\pi}{4} - 2 = $ (a meaningless mix of $\pi$ and integers).


Mistake 4 — Negative angles: A negative angle just means you rotate clockwise instead of counterclockwise. $-\frac{\pi}{6}$ lands in Quadrant IV, at the same point as $\frac{11\pi}{6}$. These are coterminal.


Mistake 5 — Simplifying $\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ incorrectly: Some students write $\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$. Both are correct, but the rationalized form $\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ is standard on most assessments.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Basic Special Angle — $\sin\left(\dfrac{3\pi}{4}\right)$

Find the exact value of $\sin\left(\dfrac{3\pi}{4}\right)$.

Step 1: Identify the quadrant.
$\frac{3\pi}{4}$ is between $\frac{\pi}{2}$ and $\pi$, so it is in Quadrant II.

Step 2: Find the reference angle.
$\theta' = \pi - \frac{3\pi}{4} = \frac{4\pi}{4} - \frac{3\pi}{4} = \frac{\pi}{4}$

Step 3: Find the base value.
$\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$

Step 4: Apply the quadrant sign.
In Quadrant II, sine is positive.

Final Answer: $\sin\left(\dfrac{3\pi}{4}\right) = \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$

Example 2: Negative Angle — $\cos\left(-\dfrac{5\pi}{6}\right)$

Find the exact value of $\cos\left(-\dfrac{5\pi}{6}\right)$.

Step 1: Find a positive coterminal angle.
$-\frac{5\pi}{6} + 2\pi = -\frac{5\pi}{6} + \frac{12\pi}{6} = \frac{7\pi}{6}$

Step 2: Identify the quadrant.
$\frac{7\pi}{6}$ is between $\pi$ and $\frac{3\pi}{2}$, so it lies in Quadrant III.

Step 3: Find the reference angle.
$\theta' = \frac{7\pi}{6} - \pi = \frac{7\pi}{6} - \frac{6\pi}{6} = \frac{\pi}{6}$

Step 4: Find the base value.
$\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{6}\right) = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$

Step 5: Apply the quadrant sign.
In Quadrant III, cosine is negative.

Final Answer: $\cos\left(-\dfrac{5\pi}{6}\right) = -\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$

Example 3: Coterminal Angle Reduction — $\tan\left(\dfrac{17\pi}{6}\right)$

Find the exact value of $\tan\left(\dfrac{17\pi}{6}\right)$.

Step 1: Reduce using coterminal reasoning.
$\frac{17\pi}{6} - 2\pi = \frac{17\pi}{6} - \frac{12\pi}{6} = \frac{5\pi}{6}$
So $\frac{17\pi}{6}$ is coterminal with $\frac{5\pi}{6}$.

Step 2: Identify the quadrant.
$\frac{5\pi}{6}$ is between $\frac{\pi}{2}$ and $\pi$ — Quadrant II.

Step 3: Find the reference angle.
$\theta' = \pi - \frac{5\pi}{6} = \frac{\pi}{6}$

Step 4: Find the base value.
$\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{6}\right) = \frac{\sin(\pi/6)}{\cos(\pi/6)} = \frac{1/2}{\sqrt{3}/2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}$

Step 5: Apply the quadrant sign.
In Quadrant II, tangent is negative.

Final Answer: $\tan\left(\dfrac{17\pi}{6}\right) = -\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3}$

Example 4: All Six Functions — $\theta = \dfrac{5\pi}{3}$

Evaluate all six trigonometric functions for $\theta = \dfrac{5\pi}{3}$.

Step 1: Identify the quadrant and reference angle.
$\frac{5\pi}{3}$ is between $\frac{3\pi}{2}$ and $2\pi$ — Quadrant IV.
Reference angle: $\theta' = 2\pi - \frac{5\pi}{3} = \frac{6\pi}{3} - \frac{5\pi}{3} = \frac{\pi}{3}$

Step 2: Read the base coordinate.
At $\frac{\pi}{3}$: $\cos = \frac{1}{2}$, $\sin = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$

Step 3: Apply Quadrant IV signs (cosine $+$, sine $-$):
$\cos\left(\frac{5\pi}{3}\right) = \dfrac{1}{2}$, $\quad \sin\left(\frac{5\pi}{3}\right) = -\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$

Step 4: Compute tangent and reciprocals.

$$\tan\theta = \frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta} = \frac{-\sqrt{3}/2}{1/2} = -\sqrt{3}$$ $$\csc\theta = \frac{1}{\sin\theta} = \frac{1}{-\sqrt{3}/2} = -\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}} = -\frac{2\sqrt{3}}{3}$$ $$\sec\theta = \frac{1}{\cos\theta} = \frac{1}{1/2} = 2$$ $$\cot\theta = \frac{1}{\tan\theta} = \frac{1}{-\sqrt{3}} = -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}$$

Final Answers:
$\sin = -\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$, $\cos = \dfrac{1}{2}$, $\tan = -\sqrt{3}$, $\csc = -\dfrac{2\sqrt{3}}{3}$, $\sec = 2$, $\cot = -\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3}$

Example 5 (Extended): Large Negative Angle — $\sin\left(-\dfrac{23\pi}{4}\right)$

Find the exact value of $\sin\left(-\dfrac{23\pi}{4}\right)$.

Step 1: Add multiples of $2\pi$ until the angle is in $[0, 2\pi)$.
$-\frac{23\pi}{4} + 2\pi = -\frac{23\pi}{4} + \frac{8\pi}{4} = -\frac{15\pi}{4}$
$-\frac{15\pi}{4} + 2\pi = -\frac{15\pi}{4} + \frac{8\pi}{4} = -\frac{7\pi}{4}$
$-\frac{7\pi}{4} + 2\pi = -\frac{7\pi}{4} + \frac{8\pi}{4} = \frac{\pi}{4}$

So $-\frac{23\pi}{4}$ is coterminal with $\frac{\pi}{4}$.

Step 2: Quadrant and sign.
$\frac{\pi}{4}$ is in Quadrant I — sine is positive.

Step 3: Read the value.
$\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$

Final Answer: $\sin\left(-\dfrac{23\pi}{4}\right) = \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$

Key insight: Adding $2\pi$ three times ($\frac{24\pi}{4} = 6\pi$) in one step works too: $-\frac{23\pi}{4} + \frac{24\pi}{4} = \frac{\pi}{4}$. Always look for the fastest path!

Summary of Key Concepts

  • The unit circle has radius 1, centered at the origin. Every point is $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$.
  • Cosine is the x-coordinate; sine is the y-coordinate. Never reverse these.
  • You only need to memorize three Quadrant I pairs: at $\frac{\pi}{6}$, $\frac{\pi}{4}$, and $\frac{\pi}{3}$. Symmetry fills in the rest.
  • The ASTC rule tells you which functions are positive in each quadrant (All Students Take Calculus).
  • The reference angle is the acute angle to the nearest x-axis. It gives you the magnitude; the quadrant gives you the sign.
  • Coterminal angles land at the same point. Subtract $2\pi$ (as a fraction with matching denominator) to reduce large angles.
  • Negative angles rotate clockwise. Add $2\pi$ to convert to a positive coterminal angle.
  • The reciprocal functions (csc, sec, cot) are just $\frac{1}{\sin}$, $\frac{1}{\cos}$, $\frac{1}{\tan}$ — compute the primary function first, then flip.
  • $\tan\theta$ is undefined when $\cos\theta = 0$ (at $\frac{\pi}{2}$ and $\frac{3\pi}{2}$).
