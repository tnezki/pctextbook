SECTION 6.D
Determine Key Features of Polynomial Functions

Every polynomial function has distinctive features that define its character: its degree, the behavior of its roots, where it intersects the axes, and how it behaves at extreme values. By identifying these features, you create a complete picture of the function's behavior. This section consolidates everything you've learned into a systematic process for analyzing any polynomial.

Learning Objectives

  • I can identify and interpret key features of polynomial functions, including degree, leading coefficient, x and y intercepts, and end behavior.
  • I can determine whether polynomial functions exhibit even or odd degree behavior from their graphs and look for regularity in patterns.
  • I can analyze the effects of degree, roots, and leading coefficient on the shape and position of polynomial graphs.

Introduction: Understanding Complete Polynomial Behavior

Analyzing a polynomial function is like understanding a person's character. You look at many features to get the complete picture. A polynomial's degree tells you its potential complexity, the leading coefficient tells you its ultimate direction, the roots tell you where it changes sides of the axis, and more. Together, these features paint a detailed portrait of how the function behaves across the entire real number line.

Real-World Connection

Climate scientists use polynomial models to analyze long-term temperature trends. By understanding the degree, intercepts, and turning points, they can predict whether temperatures will continue rising, stabilize, or cycle. Financial analysts use similar techniques to identify market trends and predict economic cycles. Understanding polynomial features helps professionals make informed predictions from mathematical models.

Historical Note

The systematic analysis of polynomial features emerged from the work of mathematicians in the 18th and 19th centuries. Isaac Newton and other pioneers developed techniques to study the complete behavior of functions, laying groundwork for modern calculus and analysis.

Key Vocabulary and Concepts

Degree: The highest power of $x$ in the polynomial. The degree determines the maximum number of roots and broadly describes the function's shape.

End Behavior: The direction the graph goes as $x \to \infty$ and $x \to -\infty$. Determined by the degree and leading coefficient.

Turning Points: Local maxima or minima where the graph changes from increasing to decreasing or vice versa. A polynomial of degree $n$ has at most $n - 1$ turning points.

Even and Odd Polynomials

A polynomial is even if it contains only even powers: $f(-x) = f(x)$. These graphs are symmetric about the y-axis. A polynomial is odd if it contains only odd powers: $f(-x) = -f(x)$. These graphs are symmetric about the origin.

Summary of Key Concepts

  • Degree determines the basic shape and the maximum number of roots and turning points.
  • The leading coefficient determines end behavior along with the degree.
  • End behavior for odd-degree polynomials: opposite directions at each end. For even-degree: same direction at both ends.
  • Roots tell you where the graph crosses or touches the x-axis.
  • The y-intercept is always $f(0)$.
  • A polynomial of degree $n$ has at most $n - 1$ turning points.
  • Even polynomials are symmetric about the y-axis.
  • Odd polynomials are symmetric about the origin.
  • Domain is all real numbers.
  • Range depends on the degree and leading coefficient.

12. Given a polynomial of degree 5 with positive leading coefficient and roots at $x = -2, 0, 3$, describe its possible graph and end behavior.

Challenge

13. Construct a polynomial of degree 4 with positive leading coefficient that has roots at $x = -1$ (multiplicity 2) and $x = 2$ (multiplicity 2).
14. Design a polynomial with three turning points and explain how you know the degree must be at least 4.
15. Create a polynomial that is odd and has three real roots. Describe its symmetry and end behavior.

Justify

16. Justify why a polynomial of degree $n$ can have at most $n - 1$ turning points.
17. Explain why even-degree polynomials can have restricted ranges while odd-degree polynomials do not.
18. Explain how the degree and leading coefficient together determine the overall shape of a polynomial graph.

Solutions

Easy

1. $f(x) = 3x^2 - 12x + 9$ Degree: 2 Leading coefficient: 3 Y-intercept: $f(0) = 9$, point $(0, 9)$

2. $f(x) = x^4 - 3x^2 + 5$ Check symmetry: $f(-x) = (-x)^4 - 3(-x)^2 + 5 = x^4 - 3x^2 + 5 = f(x)$ The function is even.

3. $f(x) = -x^3 + 6x$ Degree: 3 (odd) Leading coefficient: -1 (negative) End behavior: As $x \to -\infty$, $f(x) \to +\infty$ As $x \to +\infty$, $f(x) \to -\infty$

4. A polynomial of degree 6 has at most $6 - 1 = 5$ turning points.

Medium

5. $f(x) = -x^3 + 6x$ Factor: $f(x) = -x(x^2 - 6) = -x(x - \sqrt{6})(x + \sqrt{6})$ X-intercepts: $x = 0, \pm \sqrt{6}$ Y-intercept: $(0, 0)$ End behavior: up-left, down-right

6. Degree 4 (even) with positive leading coefficient Both ends go upward.

7. $f(x) = (x + 2)^2(x - 1)$ Roots: $x = -2$ (multiplicity 2, bounces) $x = 1$ (multiplicity 1, crosses)

8. $f(x) = x^5 - 5x^3 + 4x$ $f(-x) = -x^5 + 5x^3 - 4x = -f(x)$ The function is odd.

Hard

9. $f(x) = 2x^4 - 8x^2$ Factor: $2x^2(x^2 - 4) = 2x^2(x - 2)(x + 2)$ Degree: 4 Leading coefficient: 2 (positive) X-intercepts: $x = 0$ (multiplicity 2), $x = \pm 2$ Y-intercept: $(0, 0)$ End behavior: both ends up Symmetry: even function Turning points: at most 3

10. Roots: -3, 1, 4 (cross), and 0 (bounce) Minimum multiplicities: 1 + 1 + 1 + 2 = 5 But both ends go downward → even degree Minimum even degree satisfying multiplicities: 6

11. $f(x) = (x - 1)^2(x^2 - 4) = (x - 1)^2(x - 2)(x + 2)$ Degree: 4 Leading coefficient: positive Roots: 1 (bounce), 2, -2 (cross) Y-intercept: $f(0) = (−1)^2(−4) = -4$ End behavior: both ends up

12. Degree 5 (odd), positive leading coefficient End behavior: $x \to -\infty$, $f(x) \to -\infty$ $x \to +\infty$, $f(x) \to +\infty$ Graph crosses at -2, 0, 3.

Challenge

13. $f(x) = (x + 1)^2(x - 2)^2$ Degree: 4 Both ends up Bounces at both roots

14. A polynomial with 3 turning points must have degree at least 4 because a degree $n$ polynomial has at most $n - 1$ turning points.

15. Example: $f(x) = x(x - 2)(x + 3)$ Degree 3 (odd) Odd symmetry if constructed properly End behavior: down-left, up-right

Justify

16. A polynomial of degree $n$ can have at most $n - 1$ turning points because each turning point corresponds to a change in direction, which relates to critical points derived from the derivative of degree $n - 1$.

17. Even-degree polynomials have the same end behavior on both sides, which creates a minimum or maximum that restricts the range. Odd-degree polynomials extend infinitely in opposite directions, so their range is all real numbers.

18. The degree determines whether the graph behaves like an even or odd function overall, and the leading coefficient determines whether the ends go up or down. Together, they define the global shape of the polynomial.
