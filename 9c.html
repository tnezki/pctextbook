9.C Graph Reciprocal Functions

Reciprocal functions are defined by division and behave differently from polynomial functions. Their graphs are shaped by asymptotes and symmetry rather than intercepts and turning points.
“Reciprocal graphs are defined by what they approach, not what they reach.”

Learning Targets

I can graph basic reciprocal functions and identify key features.

I can identify asymptotes and intercepts.

I can describe how transformations affect reciprocal graphs.

Introduction

The parent reciprocal function is:

$$ f(x)=\frac{1}{x} $$

Unlike polynomial functions, reciprocal functions are not continuous. The graph is split into separate branches by a vertical asymptote, and it approaches—but never touches—both vertical and horizontal asymptotes.

Key Idea: Reciprocal functions are defined by asymptotes and symmetry, not by intercepts or turning points.

History

Reciprocal relationships have been studied since ancient mathematics, where ratios were used to compare quantities. These early ideas laid the foundation for functions defined by division.

With the development of coordinate geometry in the 17th century, mathematicians such as René Descartes began graphing functions like $\frac{1}{x}$, revealing their distinct structure and asymptotic behavior.

Today, reciprocal functions are used in science and engineering to model inverse relationships and systems that approach limits without reaching them.

Key Concepts

Parent Reciprocal Function

$$ f(x)=\frac{1}{x} $$

The graph of $ \frac{1}{x} $ consists of two branches and is defined for all real values except $x=0$.

Asymptotes

Reciprocal functions have two asymptotes that define their structure:

  • Vertical Asymptote: $x=0$
  • Horizontal Asymptote: $y=0$

The graph approaches these lines but never touches them.

Intercepts

Reciprocal functions do not have an x-intercept because the function never equals zero.

There is also no y-intercept because the function is undefined at $x=0$.

Key Observation:

The graph of a reciprocal function has no intercepts.

Symmetry and Orientation

The graph of $ \frac{1}{x} $ is symmetric about the origin.

  • One branch lies in Quadrant I
  • The other branch lies in Quadrant III

Transformations

$$ f(x)=\frac{a}{x-h}+k $$
  • $h$ shifts the graph left or right
  • $k$ shifts the graph up or down
  • $a$ controls stretch and reflection
Key Idea: Reciprocal graphs are defined by asymptotes and symmetry. Transformations move the graph but do not change its fundamental structure.

Vocabulary

Reciprocal Function:

A function of the form $ f(x)=\frac{1}{x} $, where the variable appears in the denominator.

Asymptote:

A line that the graph approaches but never touches.

Vertical Asymptote:

A vertical line where the function is undefined and the graph approaches without crossing.

Horizontal Asymptote:

A horizontal line that describes the end behavior of the function as $x$ becomes large.

Symmetry:

A property where a graph is unchanged under a transformation, such as reflection about the origin.

Branch:

One part of a graph separated by a vertical asymptote.

Strategies

Graphing Reciprocal Functions:

  1. Identify asymptotes first.
    Determine the vertical and horizontal asymptotes before drawing the graph.
  2. Determine orientation.
    Use the sign of the function to decide which quadrants the branches will lie in.
  3. Apply transformations.
    Shift the graph according to $h$ and $k$, and adjust for reflections or stretches using $a$.
  4. Sketch branches near asymptotes.
    Draw the graph approaching the asymptotes without touching them.
  5. Check for intercepts.
    Verify whether intercepts exist based on the structure of the function.

Common Mistakes

  • Crossing asymptotes:
    Reciprocal graphs approach asymptotes but never touch or cross them.
  • Incorrect orientation:
    Forgetting that a negative sign reflects the graph across the x-axis, changing which quadrants the branches occupy.
  • Misidentifying asymptotes:
    Students often forget to shift asymptotes when $h$ and $k$ are present.
  • Expecting intercepts:
    Reciprocal functions typically do not have x- or y-intercepts.
  • Graphing like a polynomial:
    Reciprocal functions are not continuous curves—they have separate branches.

Summary

  • Reciprocal functions are defined by asymptotes and symmetry.
  • The parent function $\\frac{1}{x}$ has asymptotes at $x=0$ and $y=0$.
  • Transformations shift asymptotes and change orientation.
  • The graph consists of two branches separated by a vertical asymptote.
  • Understanding structure allows accurate graphing without plotting many points.

Detailed Example Solutions

Example 1 — Parent Function

$$ f(x)=\frac{1}{x} $$

The vertical asymptote is $x=0$ and the horizontal asymptote is $y=0$. The graph has two branches in Quadrants I and III.

Example 2 — Horizontal Shift

$$ f(x)=\frac{1}{x-2} $$

The graph shifts right 2 units, so the vertical asymptote is $x=2$ and the horizontal asymptote remains $y=0$.

Example 3 — Vertical Shift

$$ f(x)=\frac{1}{x}+3 $$

The graph shifts upward 3 units. The vertical asymptote remains $x=0$ and the horizontal asymptote becomes $y=3$.

Example 4 — Reflection

$$ f(x)=-\frac{1}{x} $$

The negative sign reflects the graph across the x-axis, moving branches to Quadrants II and IV.

Example 5 — Vertical Stretch

$$ f(x)=\frac{2}{x} $$

The graph is stretched vertically, making it steeper, but asymptotes remain unchanged.

Example 6 — Combined Transformations

$$ f(x)=\frac{1}{x+4}-1 $$

The graph shifts left 4 units and down 1 unit. The asymptotes become $x=-4$ and $y=-1$.

Example 7 — Reflection and Translation

$$ f(x)=-\frac{3}{x-1}+2 $$

The graph shifts right 1 unit and up 2 units. The negative sign reflects the graph and the factor of 3 creates a vertical stretch.
