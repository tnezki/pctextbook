SECTION 7.E

Riemann Sums and Trapezoidal Approximation

What is the area of a region bounded by a curve — not a straight line, but a curve that bends and changes? This question puzzled mathematicians for over two thousand years. The answer came from a surprisingly simple idea: fill the region with rectangles, add up their areas, and then use more and more rectangles until the approximation becomes as accurate as you need. This section introduces that idea — the foundation upon which all of integral calculus is built.
“ The infinite is reached not in one great leap, but in the accumulation of many small, careful steps. ”

Learning Objectives

  • I can approximate the area under a curve using left and right Riemann sums from graphs and tables.
  • I can apply the trapezoidal rule to approximate area under a curve.
  • I can compare approximation methods and justify which provides a better estimate based on the behavior of the function.
  • I can model real-world situations involving accumulation and interpret units correctly.

Introduction: Measuring What Cannot Be Measured Exactly

Consider a simple question: how far does a car travel in 10 seconds if it is constantly accelerating? You cannot just multiply speed times time, because the speed is always changing. What you need is the area under the velocity curve — and that area represents total distance traveled.

This is the central idea of this section. When a quantity changes continuously — velocity, rainfall, electrical power, medication concentration — the total accumulated amount is represented by the area under a graph. For straight-line graphs, this area is easy to compute with geometry. For curves, we need a different approach.

The strategy is elegantly simple: divide the interval into small pieces, approximate the area of each piece using a rectangle or trapezoid, and add them all up. The approximation improves as the pieces get smaller. This process is called a Riemann sum, named after the 19th-century mathematician Bernhard Riemann who formalized it — though the underlying idea was used by Archimedes nearly 2,000 years earlier.

There are three variations you will learn: the left Riemann sum (rectangle heights from the left edge), the right Riemann sum (rectangle heights from the right edge), and the trapezoidal rule (trapezoids that connect both edges). Each has predictable over- and underestimation behavior based on whether the function is increasing or decreasing — understanding that behavior is just as important as computing the sums.

🌎 Real-World Application — Distance from Velocity:

The area under this velocity–time graph gives the total distance traveled. Because velocity is always changing, we cannot just multiply — we must approximate using rectangles or trapezoids. This is exactly the setup for a Riemann sum.
📚 Historical Note: Archimedes of Syracuse (c. 287–212 BC) used a method called "exhaustion" to approximate the area of a circle and a parabolic region by inscribing polygons with more and more sides — the same core idea as Riemann sums. Bernhard Riemann (1826–1866) formalized the definition of the definite integral using these sums in the 1850s, giving calculus the rigorous foundation it needed. Isaac Newton and Gottfried Leibniz had independently invented calculus roughly 200 years earlier, but it was Riemann who made it mathematically airtight.
🔗 Connections to Other Subjects and Real Life:

Physics — Kinematics: The area under a velocity–time graph is displacement. The area under an acceleration–time graph is the change in velocity. These relationships are foundational to all of classical mechanics.

Medicine — Pharmacokinetics: The area under a drug concentration–time curve (called AUC) tells doctors how much of a drug the body absorbs. Riemann sums are used to estimate AUC from discrete blood sample measurements.

Environmental Science: Total rainfall during a storm, total carbon emitted over a year, or total heat absorbed by the ocean — all are computed as areas under curves approximated by Riemann sums from sensor data.

Calculus — The Definite Integral: The definite integral $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ is defined as the limit of Riemann sums as the number of subintervals approaches infinity. This entire section is a preview of that concept.

Key Vocabulary and Concepts

Riemann Sum: An approximation of the area under a curve on an interval $[a, b]$ computed by dividing the interval into $n$ equal subintervals of width $\Delta x = \frac{b-a}{n}$, constructing a rectangle on each subinterval, and summing the rectangle areas. The height of each rectangle is determined by the function value at a chosen point in the subinterval.
Left Riemann Sum ($L_n$): Each rectangle's height is the function value at the left endpoint of its subinterval. If the function is increasing, the left sum underestimates the true area. If the function is decreasing, it overestimates. $$L_n = \Delta x \left[f(x_0) + f(x_1) + f(x_2) + \cdots + f(x_{n-1})\right]$$
Right Riemann Sum ($R_n$): Each rectangle's height is the function value at the right endpoint of its subinterval. If the function is increasing, the right sum overestimates. If decreasing, it underestimates. $$R_n = \Delta x \left[f(x_1) + f(x_2) + f(x_3) + \cdots + f(x_n)\right]$$
Trapezoidal Rule ($T_n$): Instead of rectangles, each subinterval uses a trapezoid whose parallel sides are the function values at the left and right endpoints. This averages the left and right sums and is generally more accurate than either alone. $$T_n = \frac{\Delta x}{2}\left[f(x_0) + 2f(x_1) + 2f(x_2) + \cdots + 2f(x_{n-1}) + f(x_n)\right]$$ Equivalently: $T_n = \dfrac{L_n + R_n}{2}$
Subinterval Width ($\Delta x$): The width of each equal subdivision of $[a, b]$: $$\Delta x = \frac{b - a}{n}$$ where $n$ is the number of subintervals. This is the width of every rectangle or trapezoid.

The Partition Points

When you divide $[a, b]$ into $n$ subintervals, you create $n + 1$ partition points:

$$x_0 = a, \quad x_1 = a + \Delta x, \quad x_2 = a + 2\Delta x, \quad \ldots, \quad x_n = b$$
  • Left sum uses: $x_0, x_1, \ldots, x_{n-1}$ (all but the last)
  • Right sum uses: $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$ (all but the first)
  • Trapezoidal uses: all $n+1$ points, with interior points doubled

⚠ Common Mistakes to Avoid

Mistake 1 — Forgetting to multiply by $\Delta x$: The function values $f(x_i)$ are the heights of the rectangles. Area = height × width. You must multiply the sum of heights by $\Delta x$. Forgetting this gives an answer that is off by a factor of $\Delta x$.


Mistake 2 — Using the wrong endpoints: Left sum uses $x_0$ through $x_{n-1}$ (skip the last point). Right sum uses $x_1$ through $x_n$ (skip the first point). Students commonly use all $n+1$ points for both, which is incorrect.


Mistake 3 — Ignoring units: The units of the area are (units of $f$) × (units of $x$). For a velocity–time graph, area has units of (miles/hour) × (hours) = miles. Always label your final answer with correct units and interpret what it represents in context.


Mistake 4 — Wrong over/under estimate conclusion: The over/underestimate behavior depends on whether the function is increasing or decreasing on the interval — not on whether individual values are large or small. For an increasing function, left always underestimates and right always overestimates, regardless of the specific values.


Mistake 5 — Trapezoidal formula errors: In the trapezoidal formula, the interior points ($x_1$ through $x_{n-1}$) are each multiplied by 2, but the endpoints $x_0$ and $x_n$ are used only once. Students often forget to double the interior values or accidentally double the endpoints as well.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Left Riemann Sum — $f(x) = x^2 + 1$ on $[0, 4]$, $n = 4$

Approximate the area under $f(x) = x^2 + 1$ on $[0, 4]$ using a left Riemann sum with 4 subintervals.

Step 1: Compute $\Delta x$.

$$\Delta x = \frac{b - a}{n} = \frac{4 - 0}{4} = 1$$

Step 2: Identify the left endpoints.
$x_0 = 0,\; x_1 = 1,\; x_2 = 2,\; x_3 = 3$   (use all but the last point)

Step 3: Evaluate $f$ at each left endpoint.

$x$$f(x) = x^2 + 1$
$0$$0^2 + 1 = 1$
$1$$1^2 + 1 = 2$
$2$$2^2 + 1 = 5$
$3$$3^2 + 1 = 10$

Step 4: Multiply by $\Delta x$ and sum.

$$L_4 = \Delta x \cdot [f(0) + f(1) + f(2) + f(3)] = 1 \cdot [1 + 2 + 5 + 10] = 18$$

Final Answer: $L_4 = 18$

Note: Since $f(x) = x^2 + 1$ is increasing on $[0, 4]$, the left sum underestimates the true area. The exact area is $\frac{76}{3} \approx 25.33$, confirming this.

Example 2: Right Riemann Sum — $f(x) = x^2 + 1$ on $[0, 4]$, $n = 4$

Approximate the same area using a right Riemann sum with 4 subintervals.

Step 1: $\Delta x = 1$ (same as before).

Step 2: Right endpoints are $x_1, x_2, x_3, x_4$:
$x_1 = 1,\; x_2 = 2,\; x_3 = 3,\; x_4 = 4$

Step 3: Evaluate $f$ at each right endpoint.

$x$$f(x) = x^2 + 1$
$1$$2$
$2$$5$
$3$$10$
$4$$17$

Step 4: Apply the formula.

$$R_4 = 1 \cdot [2 + 5 + 10 + 17] = 34$$

Final Answer: $R_4 = 34$

Since $f$ is increasing, $R_4 > $ true area. We have $L_4 = 18 <$ true area $\approx 25.33 <$ $R_4 = 34$. ✓

Example 3: Trapezoidal Rule — $f(x) = x^2 + 1$ on $[0, 4]$, $n = 4$

Apply the trapezoidal rule to the same function and compare results.

Step 1: Use all 5 partition points: $0, 1, 2, 3, 4$.

Step 2: Apply the trapezoidal formula.
Interior points ($x_1, x_2, x_3$) are doubled; endpoints ($x_0, x_4$) are not.

$$T_4 = \frac{\Delta x}{2}\left[f(0) + 2f(1) + 2f(2) + 2f(3) + f(4)\right]$$ $$= \frac{1}{2}\left[1 + 2(2) + 2(5) + 2(10) + 17\right]$$ $$= \frac{1}{2}\left[1 + 4 + 10 + 20 + 17\right] = \frac{52}{2} = 26$$

Final Answer: $T_4 = 26$

Verify: $T_4 = \frac{L_4 + R_4}{2} = \frac{18 + 34}{2} = 26$ ✓
The trapezoidal rule gives $26$, much closer to the true value of $\approx 25.33$ than either $L_4$ or $R_4$ alone.

Example 4: From a Table — Velocity Data

A car's velocity $v(t)$ (in mph) is recorded in the table below. Approximate the total distance traveled on $[0, 10]$ using left and right Riemann sums with 5 subintervals.

$t$ (sec)0246810
$v(t)$ (mph)202834394346

Step 1: Identify $\Delta x$.
$\Delta x = \frac{10-0}{5} = 2$ seconds per subinterval.

Step 2: Left sum (use $t = 0, 2, 4, 6, 8$).

$$L_5 = 2[v(0) + v(2) + v(4) + v(6) + v(8)] = 2[20 + 28 + 34 + 39 + 43] = 2(164) = 328$$

Step 3: Right sum (use $t = 2, 4, 6, 8, 10$).

$$R_5 = 2[v(2) + v(4) + v(6) + v(8) + v(10)] = 2[28 + 34 + 39 + 43 + 46] = 2(190) = 380$$

Step 4: Trapezoidal estimate.

$$T_5 = \frac{L_5 + R_5}{2} = \frac{328 + 380}{2} = 354$$

Final Answers: $L_5 = 328$ mph·sec, $R_5 = 380$ mph·sec, $T_5 = 354$ mph·sec

Interpretation: Since velocity is increasing, $L_5$ underestimates and $R_5$ overestimates total distance. The trapezoidal rule gives the best estimate: approximately 354 mph·sec of distance. (Note: converting to miles requires unit adjustment — this illustrates why units matter.)

Example 5 (Extended): Comparing Methods — $f(x) = 5 - x^2$ on $[-3, 3]$, $n = 6$

Approximate the area using left and right Riemann sums, then determine whether they over- or underestimate.

Step 1: Compute $\Delta x$.

$$\Delta x = \frac{3 - (-3)}{6} = 1$$

Step 2: Partition points: $-3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3$.

Step 3: Evaluate $f(x) = 5 - x^2$ at all points.

$x$$-3$$-2$$-1$$0$$1$$2$$3$
$f(x)$$-4$$1$$4$$5$$4$$1$$-4$

Step 4: Left sum (omit $x = 3$).

$$L_6 = 1 \cdot [-4 + 1 + 4 + 5 + 4 + 1] = 11$$

Step 5: Right sum (omit $x = -3$).

$$R_6 = 1 \cdot [1 + 4 + 5 + 4 + 1 + (-4)] = 11$$

Step 6: Interpret.
Both sums equal 11. This is because $f(x) = 5 - x^2$ is symmetric about the y-axis on $[-3, 3]$. The function increases on $[-3, 0]$ and decreases on $[0, 3]$, so the over- and underestimation errors from each half cancel exactly.

Final Answer: $L_6 = R_6 = 11$. The trapezoidal rule also gives $T_6 = 11$.

Example 6 (Extended): Concavity and Accuracy

For $f(x) = x^2 + 4$ on $[1, 6]$ with $n = 10$, explain without computing which method gives the closest estimate to the true area and why.

Step 1: Determine the behavior of $f$.
$f(x) = x^2 + 4$ is increasing on $[1, 6]$ (derivative $= 2x > 0$) and concave up (second derivative $= 2 > 0$).

Step 2: Predict left and right sum errors.
Increasing → Left underestimates, Right overestimates.

Step 3: Analyze the trapezoidal rule.
For a concave-up function, the straight-line chord connecting two points on the curve lies above the curve. The trapezoidal rule uses these chords, so it overestimates — but by less than the right sum.

Step 4: Rank the estimates.

$$L_{10} < \text{true area} < T_{10} < R_{10}$$

Conclusion: The trapezoidal rule $T_{10}$ is closest to the true value because it balances the left and right errors, even though it still overestimates for a concave-up function.

Summary of Key Concepts

  • Riemann sums approximate the area under a curve by summing the areas of rectangles whose heights come from function values.
  • The subinterval width is $\Delta x = \frac{b-a}{n}$. Always compute this first.
  • Left sum: uses $f(x_0)$ through $f(x_{n-1})$ — skips the last point.
  • Right sum: uses $f(x_1)$ through $f(x_n)$ — skips the first point.
  • Trapezoidal rule: uses all $n+1$ points, doubling interior values. Equals $\frac{L_n + R_n}{2}$.
  • For an increasing function: $L_n$ underestimates, $R_n$ overestimates.
  • For a decreasing function: $L_n$ overestimates, $R_n$ underestimates.
  • The trapezoidal rule is generally more accurate because it averages the left and right errors.
  • For concave-up functions, the trapezoidal rule overestimates. For concave-down, it underestimates.
  • More subintervals = smaller $\Delta x$ = better approximation. As $n \to \infty$, Riemann sums converge to the definite integral.
  • Units matter: area units = (function units) × (x-variable units).
  • The area under a velocity–time graph represents distance. This connection is the heart of calculus.
