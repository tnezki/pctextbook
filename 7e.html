SECTION 7.E
Above & Beyond: Riemann Sums and Trapezoidal Approximation

Learning Objectives

I can approximate the area under a curve using left and right Riemann sums from graphs and tables.

I can apply trapezoidal sums to approximate area.

I can compare approximation methods and justify which provides a better estimate.

I can model real-world situations involving accumulation.

Real-World Application

Many real-world quantities cannot be measured exactly.

  • Total rainfall during a storm.
  • Distance traveled when speed changes continuously.
  • Total energy used over time.
  • Accumulated medication levels in the bloodstream.

Suppose a car’s velocity (in miles per hour) is recorded every 2 seconds.

We want to estimate the total distance traveled over 10 seconds.

Because velocity is changing, we cannot multiply by a single constant speed.

Instead, we approximate the area under the velocity curve.

The area under a velocity–time graph represents distance traveled.

This is the foundation of calculus.

Key Vocabulary

Riemann Sum: Approximation of area using rectangles.

Left Riemann Sum: Uses left endpoints.

Right Riemann Sum: Uses right endpoints.

Trapezoidal Rule: Uses trapezoids to approximate area.

Core Concepts

Increasing → Left underestimates, Right overestimates.

Decreasing → Left overestimates, Right underestimates.

Trapezoidal averages endpoint behavior.

Structure Insight

  • We estimate area numerically.
  • Units matter.
  • More subintervals improve accuracy.
  • We justify reasoning, not just compute.

Summary

  • Riemann sums approximate area.
  • Left and right behave differently.
  • Trapezoidal improves accuracy.
  • Increasing subintervals improves precision.

Common Mistakes

  • Forgetting to multiply by $\Delta x$.
  • Mixing left and right endpoints.
  • Ignoring units.
  • Failing to interpret over/under estimates.

Practice Problems

Easy

1. Use 6 subintervals to approximate the area under $f(x)=5-x^2$ on $[-3,3]$ using a left Riemann sum.
2. Use 6 subintervals to approximate the area under $f(x)=5-x^2$ on $[-3,3]$ using a right Riemann sum.
3. Determine whether the left Riemann sum in Problem 1 is an overestimate or underestimate. Justify using function behavior.
4. Compute $\Delta x$ for 6 subintervals on $[-3,3]$. Explain what it represents geometrically.

Medium

5. Use 4 subintervals to approximate the area under $f(x)=2^x+3$ on $[1,3]$ using a left Riemann sum.
6. Use 4 subintervals to approximate the area under $f(x)=2^x+3$ on $[1,3]$ using a right Riemann sum.
7. Use 4 subintervals to approximate the area under $f(x)=2^x+3$ on $[1,3]$ using the trapezoidal rule.
8. Compare your answers from 5–7. Which method gives the largest estimate? Explain why.

Hard

9. Use 10 subintervals to approximate the area under $f(x)=x^2+4$ on $[1,6]$ using a left Riemann sum.
10. Use 10 subintervals to approximate the area under $f(x)=x^2+4$ on $[1,6]$ using a right Riemann sum.
11. Use 10 subintervals to approximate the area under $f(x)=x^2+4$ on $[1,6]$ using the trapezoidal rule.
12. Compare Problems 9–11. Which approximation should be closest to the true value? Explain using concavity.

Challenge

13. A velocity function is increasing and concave up on an interval. Predict which method (left, right, trapezoidal) will give the smallest estimate. Justify.
14. A function is decreasing and concave down. Predict which method will give the largest estimate. Explain.
15. Without calculating exact values, explain how doubling the number of subintervals affects approximation error.

Justify

16. Explain why increasing the number of subintervals improves approximation accuracy.
17. Justify why the trapezoidal rule is often more accurate than a left or right Riemann sum.
18. Explain how Riemann sums connect to the definition of a definite integral in calculus.

Solutions

Easy

1. $\Delta x = \frac{3-(-3)}{6} = 1$ Left endpoints: $-3,-2,-1,0,1,2$ $f(x)=5-x^2$ Values: $f(-3)=-4$ $f(-2)=1$ $f(-1)=4$ $f(0)=5$ $f(1)=4$ $f(2)=1$ Left sum: $L = 1(-4+1+4+5+4+1)=11$

2. Right endpoints: $-2,-1,0,1,2,3$ Values: $1,4,5,4,1,-4$ Right sum: $R = 1(1+4+5+4+1-4)=11$

3. Because $5-x^2$ is symmetric about the y-axis, left and right sums balance. Neither strictly over- nor underestimates over symmetric interval.

4. $\Delta x = 1$ This represents the width of each rectangle.

Medium

5. $\Delta x = \frac{3-1}{4}=0.5$ Left endpoints: 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5 $f(x)=2^x+3$ $f(1)=5$ $f(1.5)=2^{1.5}+3≈5.828$ $f(2)=7$ $f(2.5)=2^{2.5}+3≈8.657$ $L ≈ 0.5(5+5.828+7+8.657)=13.2425$

6. Right endpoints: 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3 $f(3)=11$ $R ≈ 0.5(5.828+7+8.657+11)=16.7425$

7. $T = \frac{L+R}{2} ≈ \frac{13.2425+16.7425}{2} = 14.9925$

8. Function increasing and concave up. Left underestimates, right overestimates, trapezoidal slightly overestimates but closest.

Hard

9. $\Delta x = \frac{6-1}{10}=0.5$ Left endpoints: 1,1.5,2,…,5.5 Compute each and multiply by 0.5 Left sum ≈ 108.125

10. Right endpoints: 1.5,2,…,6 Right sum ≈ 133.125

11. $T = \frac{108.125+133.125}{2}=120.625$

12. Since $x^2+4$ is increasing and concave up: Left underestimates, right overestimates, trapezoidal closest but slightly over.

Challenge

13. Increasing & concave up → Left gives smallest estimate.

14. Decreasing & concave down → Left gives largest estimate.

15. Doubling subintervals reduces error because rectangles better approximate curvature.

Justify

16. Smaller $\Delta x$ reduces maximum gap between curve and rectangles.

17. Trapezoidal balances left and right error, canceling part of over/under estimation.

18. As $\Delta x \to 0$, Riemann sums converge to the definite integral.
